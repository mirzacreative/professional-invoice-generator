Proforma Invoice: What It Is and When to Use It (2026)

Updated 21 November 2026 · 9-minute read

Quick answer: A proforma invoice is a preliminary bill sent before goods or services are delivered. It looks like an invoice — it lists what will be supplied, in what quantity, at what price, and on what terms — but it is not the final tax invoice. Its job is to let the buyer approve the details, arrange an advance or deposit payment, clear goods through customs, or get approval inside their own organisation, before the sale is complete. When the goods or services are actually supplied, you issue a proper tax invoice with its own number. A proforma invoice should be clearly labelled "Proforma Invoice", should not be recorded as a sale, and should not be used to claim tax. This post is general information, not legal or tax advice: the rules on invoicing vary by jurisdiction, so for a specific question about what applies where you are based, check with a qualified adviser.

Freelancers and small sellers meet the proforma invoice at an awkward moment. A new client says, "Send me an invoice so I can get approval," but the work has not started. A buyer overseas asks for an invoice for customs, but nothing has shipped. A client wants to pay a deposit, but there is nothing to bill yet. In each case, a normal invoice feels wrong — because the sale has not happened — and a casual email feels too vague, because the client needs the details in a form their finance team or their customs broker will accept. That gap is exactly what a proforma invoice fills. This guide explains what a proforma invoice is, how it differs from a quote, a tax invoice, and a receipt, when to use one and when not to, what to put on it, how to number it, how it works for advance payments and for customs, and how to turn it into a real invoice when the work is done.

What a proforma invoice is — and what it is not

A proforma invoice is a document that shows, in invoice form, what a sale will look like. It carries the seller's and buyer's details, a description of the goods or services, quantities, prices, any taxes that would apply, the total, and the terms — the same information a tax invoice carries, presented before the sale is complete. The word "proforma" means "as a matter of form": the document has the form of an invoice, but it is issued before the transaction it describes. The buyer uses it to check the details, to approve them, to arrange payment, or to present it to a third party, such as a bank or a customs authority, that needs to see the expected value and contents of a shipment.

What it is not matters just as much. A proforma invoice is not a tax invoice. It does not, by itself, create a tax point, and in most cases it should not be entered in your accounts as a sale or used by the buyer to claim tax. A proforma invoice is not a receipt: a receipt proves that payment has been received, and a proforma invoice is issued before payment, or before the goods or services are supplied. Our guide on the difference between an invoice and a receipt explains that distinction in detail, and it applies here too: the proforma invoice says what will be charged, the tax invoice says what is charged, and the receipt says what was paid. And a proforma invoice is not a contract on its own, although it can record terms the two sides have agreed. If the terms matter — payment dates, delivery dates, what happens if the buyer cancels — put them in a contract or a written agreement as well, and keep the proforma invoice as the summary of the commercial details, not the whole agreement.

Proforma invoice vs quote vs tax invoice

These three documents are often confused, because they carry much of the same information. The difference is timing and purpose, not layout.

A quote or estimate comes first. It is an offer: it says what you would charge, for what, if the buyer goes ahead. A quote may be a short email, a figure in a message, or a formal document. It is the document a buyer compares against other suppliers, and it usually carries a validity period — "this price holds for 30 days" — because prices and availability change. A quote does not assume the buyer has said yes.

A proforma invoice comes next, after the buyer has agreed in principle. It takes the agreed details and lays them out in invoice form, so the buyer can approve the exact figures, arrange an advance payment, or use the document for customs or internal approval. It is more formal than a quote, and it is usually closer to the final figures, but the sale is still not complete. A proforma invoice can still change: the quantity, the price, or the delivery date may move before the final invoice, and the proforma document should say so, or at least carry a validity date.

A tax invoice comes last, when the goods or services are supplied. It is the formal record of the sale: it carries the invoice number from your tax-invoice sequence, the tax details required in your jurisdiction, and the amount the buyer must pay. It is the document you record in your accounts, and the document the buyer uses for their own tax records. If you are unsure what a tax invoice must contain, start with our guide to invoice templates for freelancers, which covers the standard fields, and then check the requirements that apply in your jurisdiction.

A simple way to remember the order is: quote to offer, proforma to confirm, tax invoice to bill. Skipping the middle step is fine for a small, simple sale — many freelancers go straight from a quote to a tax invoice, and never need a proforma invoice at all. The proforma step earns its place when a third party needs invoice-form details before the sale, or when an advance payment has to be arranged on agreed figures. If nobody needs the document before the sale, you probably do not need one.

When to use a proforma invoice

There are four common situations where a proforma invoice is the right document.

1. To confirm the scope and price before work starts. A client who has agreed to a project may want the exact figures in invoice form before they sign off, so that their finance team can check the amount, the description, and the payment terms against the agreement. A proforma invoice gives them that document without you having to bill for work that has not been done. This is common with larger clients, agencies, and organisations that need paperwork before they can raise a purchase order.

2. To request an advance or deposit payment. Many freelancers ask for a deposit — often a share of the total — before starting work. A proforma invoice lets the client pay that deposit against agreed figures, with a document to file. Be clear on the proforma invoice that it relates to an advance payment, state the amount of the advance, and state how the balance will be billed. When the work is done, the tax invoice should show the full amount, the advance already received, and the balance due, so the client is never charged twice. The payment terms you have agreed should be stated on the proforma invoice too, so the client knows when the advance is due and when the balance falls due.

3. For customs and international shipments. When goods are shipped across a border, the customs authority needs to know what is in the shipment and what it is worth, even when no sale has taken place yet — for example, when goods are sent for exhibition, for repair, or as samples, or when the sale will complete after the goods arrive. A proforma invoice is the usual document for that declaration. It should list the goods, their quantities, their value, and the reason for the shipment, and it should state clearly that it is a proforma invoice. Customs requirements vary by country and by the type of goods, so check the requirements of the destination country, and the carrier's guidance, before shipping. A proforma invoice for customs is a declaration document, not a substitute for the commercial invoice that follows the sale.

4. To get internal approval inside the buyer's organisation. Some buyers cannot pay, or cannot place an order, until a document in invoice form has been approved by a manager, a finance team, or a procurement system. A proforma invoice gives them that document, with the figures they need, before the work starts. Ask the buyer what their system needs — a purchase order number, a reference, a particular description — and include it, because a proforma invoice that does not match their system will come back for correction and cost you both time.

There is also a fifth, quieter use: as a record of what was agreed. Even when nobody asks for it, a proforma invoice sent after a call, with the scope, the price, and the terms written down, protects both sides. The client can check it and reply with corrections before work starts, and you have a dated document showing what was agreed. That habit alone prevents many disputes about scope and price.

When NOT to use a proforma invoice

A proforma invoice is the wrong document in several common situations, and using it there creates confusion rather than clarity.

Do not use a proforma invoice as the final bill after the sale. Once the goods or services are supplied, issue a tax invoice. A buyer who receives only a proforma invoice after delivery may not be able to claim tax, and your own records will be incomplete. Do not use a proforma invoice to record income. Record the sale when the tax invoice is issued, not when the proforma invoice is sent, or you will overstate your income and create a tax record that does not match reality. Do not use a proforma invoice as proof of payment. If the client has paid, send a receipt or a paid tax invoice, not a proforma document. And do not use a proforma invoice to hide a sale. Issuing proforma invoices instead of tax invoices to avoid recording income is not a paperwork choice — it is a compliance problem, and it should not be done.

There are also situations where a proforma invoice is simply unnecessary. For a small, one-off job with a client who pays on completion, a quote followed by a tax invoice is enough. For a repeat client on a fixed monthly fee, a recurring invoice is the right system, and a proforma invoice for each cycle would add paperwork without adding clarity. Use the proforma invoice when a specific need calls for it — approval, an advance, customs, or a third party — and skip it when no such need exists.

What to include on a proforma invoice

A good proforma invoice carries the same core information as a tax invoice, with two additions: the words "Proforma Invoice" in a prominent place, and a clear statement that it is not a tax invoice. That labelling is what stops the document being mistaken for a final bill. Beyond that, include:

Your details and the buyer's details. Your business name, address, and contact details, and the buyer's name and address. If either side needs a tax number, a company number, or a purchase order reference on the document, include it — ask the buyer what their system requires, rather than guessing. A reference and a date. Give the document its own reference, separate from your tax-invoice sequence (see the numbering section below), and date it. Add a validity date — "this proforma invoice is valid until" — so that both sides know how long the figures hold. A description of the goods or services. Describe each item clearly, with quantities, unit prices, and line totals. Vague descriptions — "services as discussed" — cause disputes. Write the description so that a stranger reading the document could tell what is being supplied. Taxes, discounts, and the total. Show any taxes that would apply, any discount, and the total due, in the same way the tax invoice will show them, so the buyer can check the final figure. If taxes depend on the final invoice, say so in a note, rather than presenting an estimate as a final tax figure. Payment terms. State how much is due now — the full amount, or an advance — when the balance is due, and how payment should be made. Link this to the terms you have agreed, and keep the terms consistent with the terms on the final tax invoice. Delivery or completion details. For goods, the delivery address, the expected delivery date, and any shipping terms. For services, the expected start and completion dates, and any milestones. A note on what happens next. One line is enough: "A tax invoice will be issued on delivery" or "A tax invoice will be issued on completion of the work." That line tells the buyer, and anyone else who reads the document, that this is not the final bill.

Keep the layout the same as your tax invoices, with the same business details and the same style. A proforma invoice that looks like your normal invoice, with the word "Proforma" added, is easier for the buyer to file and easier for you to convert later. Numbering a proforma invoice: keep it separate

Numbering is where proforma invoices most often cause trouble. A tax invoice sequence must be clean: one sequence, every client, no gaps, no duplicates, no renumbering. A proforma invoice that uses a number from that sequence breaks it, because the proforma document is not a tax invoice, and the final tax invoice will need a number of its own. If the proforma invoice takes number 104, and the tax invoice for the same sale also takes a number, the sequence will have a gap or a duplicate, and an auditor, an accountant, or a tax authority will ask why.

The fix is simple: give proforma invoices their own separate reference sequence, clearly different from the tax-invoice sequence. A common pattern is a prefix — "PRO-001, PRO-002" — with its own running numbers, or a reference that includes the year or the client. Whatever pattern you choose, keep it consistent, keep a list of every proforma invoice you send, and never reuse a reference. Our guide to invoice numbering explains the tax-invoice rules in detail, and the same discipline applies to the proforma sequence: one list, no duplicates, no deletions. When the sale goes ahead, issue the tax invoice with the next number in your normal invoice sequence, and note the proforma reference on it — "Ref: PRO-012" — so the two documents can be matched. Keep the proforma invoice, the tax invoice, and the record of payment together in one folder, so that anyone reading the file can follow the story from quote to payment.

Advance payments, deposits, and the final invoice

Advance payments are the most common reason a freelancer sends a proforma invoice, and they need care. When a client pays an advance against a proforma invoice, the payment is real money received, even though the sale is not yet complete. Record the payment, keep the record with the proforma invoice, and when the work is done, issue a tax invoice that shows the full amount, the advance already received, and the balance due. The client should never be charged the full amount again: the tax invoice must credit the advance, in plain figures, on the face of the invoice.

Agree the advance in writing before the proforma invoice goes out: how much, when it is due, whether it is refundable if the work does not go ahead, and when the balance falls due. Those terms belong in the contract or in a confirming email, not only on the proforma invoice. If the client pays late, follow the same steps as for any overdue amount: a polite reminder in writing, with the reference, the amount, and the due date. Our guide to chasing overdue invoices covers the follow-up steps, and our guide on how to send an invoice by email covers the subject line, the message, and the attachment, so the proforma invoice is opened and acted on rather than missed. And if the work changes after the advance is paid — the scope grows, the price changes, or the client cancels — tell the client in writing before the next document goes out, and reflect the change on the final tax invoice, not by altering the proforma invoice after the event.

Proforma invoices for customs and international sales

For goods shipped across a border, a proforma invoice is often a customs document first and a commercial document second. The customs authority, and the carrier, need to know what is being shipped, how many of each item, what each item is worth, where the goods came from, and why they are being shipped — sale, sample, repair, exhibition, or return. A proforma invoice that answers those questions, clearly labelled as proforma, is the standard way to provide that information when the commercial invoice cannot yet be issued.

Be accurate. The value declared on a proforma invoice for customs should be the true value of the goods, even when no sale has taken place and no payment will be made for that shipment — for example, samples or goods sent for repair still have a value, and declaring a token value to reduce duty is a compliance risk. Describe the goods in plain, specific terms, not in general ones: "cotton T-shirts, 50 units" is useful to a customs officer, and "clothing" is not. Include the country of origin if you know it, the reason for the shipment, and any reference the carrier or the customs broker asks for. And check the destination country's requirements before shipping: some countries require particular forms, particular wording, or additional documents alongside the proforma invoice, and the carrier's guidance is a good first check. Customs rules change, and they vary by country and by goods, so for a specific shipment, check the current requirements for that destination, or ask a customs broker.

Turning a proforma invoice into a tax invoice

When the goods are delivered or the work is complete, the proforma invoice has done its job, and the tax invoice takes over. The conversion should be straightforward if the proforma invoice was accurate: copy the agreed details onto the tax invoice, update anything that changed — the quantity actually delivered, the final price, the delivery date — and issue it with the next number in your tax-invoice sequence. Note the proforma reference on the tax invoice, so the two documents can be matched. If an advance was paid, show it as a credit, with the balance due clearly stated. If nothing changed, the tax invoice will look very like the proforma invoice, apart from the title, the number, and the tax details. That similarity is a good sign: it means the proforma invoice did its job of confirming the details before the sale.

If the details changed — the scope grew, the quantity changed, the price moved — reflect the change on the tax invoice, and explain it in one line on the invoice or in the covering message, so the client is not surprised. Do not go back and alter the proforma invoice after the event. The proforma invoice is a record of what was proposed at that date, and altering it destroys the audit trail. Keep all three documents — the quote, the proforma invoice, and the tax invoice — together, with the contract and the payment record, in one folder per client or per job. That file is what you, your accountant, or a tax authority will want to see if the sale is ever questioned.

Common mistakes with proforma invoices

Most proforma-invoice problems are labelling and numbering problems, and all of them are easy to avoid. Forgetting the word "Proforma". A document that looks exactly like a tax invoice, with no proforma labelling, will be filed as a tax invoice, and the buyer may try to claim tax on it, or pay it as if it were the final bill. Put "Proforma Invoice" in the title, and add "This is not a tax invoice" in a visible place. Using the tax-invoice number sequence. As explained above, that creates a gap or a duplicate in the sequence that will be hard to explain later. Use a separate PRO sequence. Recording the proforma invoice as a sale. Record the sale when the tax invoice is issued. Recording it at the proforma stage overstates income and creates a tax record that does not match the real transaction. Leaving out a validity date. Prices and availability change, and a proforma invoice with no expiry can be presented months later as if the figures still hold. Give every proforma invoice a validity date, and reissue it if the figures change. Being vague about the advance. If an advance is due, state the amount, the due date, and how the balance will be billed. A proforma invoice that does not say what is due now will be ignored, or paid in full by mistake. Sending a proforma invoice when a tax invoice is due. After delivery, the client needs a tax invoice, not another proforma document. Send the right document at the right stage, and the paperwork will follow the sale instead of confusing it.

Proforma invoices, in short

Use a proforma invoice when the buyer needs invoice-form details before the sale is complete: to confirm the scope and price, to arrange an advance or deposit, to clear goods through customs, or to get internal approval. Label it clearly as a proforma invoice, state that it is not a tax invoice, give it a separate reference that does not use your tax-invoice sequence, give it a validity date, and keep a copy. When the sale completes, issue a tax invoice with its own number, credit any advance already paid, and keep the quote, the proforma invoice, and the tax invoice together. Frequently asked questions

What is a proforma invoice?

A proforma invoice is a preliminary bill sent before goods or services are delivered. It shows what will be supplied, in what quantity, at what price, and on what terms, so the buyer can approve the details, arrange payment, or clear customs. It is not a demand for payment in the same way a tax invoice is, it is not proof that payment has been made, and in most cases it should not be recorded as a sale or used to claim tax. A real tax invoice is issued later, when the goods or services are actually supplied.

Is a proforma invoice the same as a quote?

No, although they look similar. A quote or estimate is an offer: it says what you would charge if the buyer goes ahead. A proforma invoice goes a step further: it is laid out like an invoice, with invoice-style details, and it is usually sent after the buyer has agreed in principle, so they can approve the exact details, arrange an advance payment, or use it for customs or internal approval. A quote can turn into a proforma invoice, and a proforma invoice can turn into a tax invoice — but each document has a different job, and the wording on the document should make clear which one it is.

When should I use a proforma invoice?

Use one when the buyer needs the details in invoice form before the sale is complete: to confirm the scope and price before work starts, to request an advance or deposit payment, to support a customs declaration for goods being shipped, or to get internal approval inside the buyer's organisation. Do not use a proforma invoice as a substitute for a tax invoice after the sale, and do not use it to record income or to claim tax. When the goods or services are supplied, issue a proper tax invoice with its own number.

Does a proforma invoice need a number?

A proforma invoice should carry a reference so both sides can talk about the same document, but it should not use a number from your tax-invoice sequence. Give it a clearly separate reference — for example, a PRO prefix with its own running sequence — and label the document Proforma Invoice, so it cannot be mistaken for a tax invoice. Keep a copy of every proforma invoice you send, and when the sale goes ahead, issue the tax invoice with the next number in your normal invoice sequence.
