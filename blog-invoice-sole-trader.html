How to Invoice as a Sole Trader: What to Include

Updated 26 November 2026 · 12-minute read

Quick answer: A sole trader invoice is a normal invoice, with one extra discipline: it must identify you clearly, show what was supplied and what is owed, state the tax treatment correctly for your jurisdiction and registration, and be kept, with its supporting records, for the period your jurisdiction requires. At minimum it needs a unique invoice number, the date, your name and contact details, the client's name and address, a clear description of the goods or services, quantities and prices, the subtotal, any tax shown separately where it applies, the total due, and the payment terms, including the due date and how to pay. This guide explains each of those fields for a sole trader, the choice between a business name and a personal name, tax and VAT notes in general terms, payment terms, and record-keeping, with an illustrative worked example. It is general information only, not legal or tax advice, and the rules on invoicing, tax registration, and record-keeping vary by jurisdiction, so check the rules where you are based and ask a qualified adviser about any specific question. The guides linked throughout this post cover the steps in depth: quote versus invoice, proforma invoices, how to write an invoice, payment terms, and hourly versus project pricing.

A sole trader is a person trading on their own account, without a separate company standing between the person and the business. That simple fact shapes the invoice in two ways. First, the invoice must make clear who the trader is, because the client is paying a person, and the tax authority will treat the income as that person's income. Second, the trader carries the record-keeping personally: there is no company office to file the invoice, and no separate set of company accounts to absorb a missing number or a lost copy. A sole trader who invoices clearly, consistently, and promptly, and who keeps a complete record, will find tax returns easier, client queries shorter, and late payments fewer. A sole trader who invoices casually — a number reused, a name that changes from one invoice to the next, a total with no breakdown, a tax line copied from another trader — will find each of those tasks harder, and will create a record that is difficult to explain later. The discipline in this guide is therefore not paperwork for its own sake. It is the habit that makes a one-person business look, to a client and to a tax authority, like a business that can be trusted.

What "Sole Trader" Means on an Invoice

The term "sole trader" describes a trading status, not a document type, and the invoice itself follows the same structure as any business invoice. What changes is the identity block: the name, address, and contact details of the person trading, the name under which the person trades if that differs from the person's own name, and any registration number that applies in the jurisdiction, such as a tax or VAT registration number where the trader is registered. The invoice should also show, where the jurisdiction requires it or where clarity demands it, the trader's bank details or payment details, so that the client can pay without asking, and the client's details, so that the invoice can be matched to the client's own records. A client who receives an invoice from a sole trader will often check two things before paying: that the name on the invoice matches the name on the quote and on the bank account, and that the amount matches the amount agreed. An invoice that passes both checks is usually paid without a query. An invoice that fails either check will be queried, and the query will delay payment.

Trading status also affects the language on the invoice, in a small but important way. A sole trader should not describe the business as a company, should not use a company registration number that does not exist, and should not use a trading name in a way that suggests a separate legal person where there is none. Use the name you trade under, consistently, on the quote, the invoice, the receipt, and the bank account, and show your own name alongside the trading name where the local rules require it or where it avoids confusion about who is to be paid. If the trading name changes — for example, if a freelancer who traded under a personal name begins to trade under a studio name — change it cleanly, from a stated invoice number onward, tell regular clients in advance, and keep a note of the change with the records, so that the sequence of invoices remains intelligible. A change of name mid-sequence, without explanation, looks like a different supplier, and a client or a tax authority may treat it as one.

Finally, sole trader status does not remove the need for a written agreement before the work starts. A quote, or a written confirmation by email, that states the deliverables, the price or rate, the payment terms, and the tax treatment, is the document the invoice follows, as our quote-versus-invoice guide explains. The invoice should never contain a surprise, because the invoice should never contain anything the quote did not already say. That rule matters more for a sole trader than for a larger business, because the sole trader has no separate accounts department to absorb a dispute, and a dispute about a bill is a dispute with the trader personally. Get the agreement in writing, invoice from the agreement, and file the two documents together, per client, per job, as the record-keeping section explains.

The Required Fields: A Field-by-Field Checklist

The checklist below covers the fields a sole trader invoice should normally show. The exact legal requirements vary by jurisdiction, and the list should be checked against the rules where you are based, but an invoice that contains every field below, filled in clearly, will satisfy most clients and most record-keeping needs, and an invoice that omits a field will usually be queried for the missing information.

Your identity. Your name, or your trading name with your own name where required, your address, and a contact method, usually an email address and a telephone number. The address should be an address at which you can be contacted about the invoice, and it should be the same address used on the quote, so that the two documents visibly come from the same supplier. Invoice number. A unique number, in one unbroken sequence, with no gaps and no repeats, as explained in our invoice numbering guide. The number is the reference the client will quote when paying and when querying, and it is the key to your own records. Invoice date and, where relevant, the supply date. The date the invoice was issued, and the date or period when the goods or services were supplied, where that differs from the invoice date. The supply date matters for tax in many jurisdictions, and it should be shown where the rules require it. Client details. The client's name and address, and, where the client is a business, the name of the person or department the invoice is addressed to. Address the invoice to the person who will pay it, not only to the business in general, where that person is known.

Description of the supply. A clear description of each item: what was supplied, in what quantity, at what price. A description that says only "services" or "work done" is not clear enough for the client to check, and it is not clear enough for your own records a year later. Name the deliverable, the date or period it relates to, and the basis of the charge — hours at a rate, a fixed price for a deliverable, or a quantity at a unit price — as the hourly-versus-project guide explains. Amounts. A line total for each item, a subtotal, any discount shown plainly, any tax shown separately with the rate and the registration number where it applies, and the total due, in one currency, stated plainly. The arithmetic must be checkable from the face of the invoice: quantities multiplied by prices, plus tax, equals the total. Payment terms. When payment is due, how it is to be made, and what happens if it is late, as explained in our payment terms guide and summarised in the payment terms section below. Bank or payment details. The details the client needs to pay, shown accurately, and checked before the invoice is sent, because a wrong account number is a payment that goes to the wrong place. References. The quote reference, the client's order reference, or the project name, where one exists, so that the client can match the invoice to the approval without asking.

Two fields deserve a particular warning. The first is the invoice number. A sole trader who restarts numbering each year, who reuses a number, or who leaves a gap, creates a record that is difficult to explain to a tax authority, and a client who is asked to pay an invoice with a number already paid will query it. Use one sequence, from the first invoice onward, and if a number must be voided, keep the voided invoice in the file, marked void, with the reason noted, rather than deleting it and reusing the number. The second is the description. A description written for the client's understanding, in plain words, with dates and quantities, is the difference between an invoice that is paid on sight and an invoice that is queried. Write the description as if you will read it in two years' time, with no memory of the job, and ask whether it would still make sense. If it would not, rewrite it before the invoice is sent.

Business Name or Personal Name: Which Name Goes on the Invoice

Many sole traders trade under a name that is not their own name: a studio name, a brand name, or a name that describes the service. That is normal, and it is usually lawful, subject to the local rules on trading names, which vary. The invoice question is practical rather than philosophical: the name on the invoice must identify the supplier clearly, must match the name on the quote, and must match the name on the bank account to which the client is asked to pay. A client asked to pay a bank account in a different name from the name on the invoice will, rightly, hesitate, and a tax authority that cannot match the invoice to the trader will ask questions. The safe course is to show the trading name prominently, as the name the client knows, and to show the trader's own name alongside it, in the identity block, where the local rules require it or where it removes doubt about who is to be paid. Use the same combination on every document — quote, invoice, receipt, and payment details — so that the client's file tells one consistent story.

The choice of trading name itself deserves care. Choose a name that describes the service plainly, that does not suggest a company where there is none, that does not copy another trader's name in a way that could confuse clients, and that can be used consistently on the website, the email address, and the invoice. Check, before adopting a name, whether another trader in the same field is already using it in the same area, and whether the name is available as a domain and as an email address, because a name that cannot be used consistently will be changed later, and a change of name mid-sequence creates the confusion described above. If a trading name is registered or notified to an authority in your jurisdiction, follow the local procedure, and show any registration details the rules require. If no registration is required, keep a record of the date the name was first used, and of the documents on which it appears, so that the history of the name can be shown if it is ever questioned. A trading name is an asset of a one-person business, and it should be treated as one: chosen deliberately, used consistently, and changed only for a good reason, with clients told in advance.

Bank details follow the same rule of consistency. The account to which the client pays should be in the name that appears on the invoice, or the difference should be explained on the invoice itself, in words, so that the client is not left to guess. A sole trader commonly uses a personal bank account, a business account in the trading name, or a dedicated account for the business, and the choice is a matter for the trader and the bank, but the invoice must reflect the choice accurately. Check the account name, the account number, and any payment reference the client should quote, before every invoice is sent, and check them again if the bank details change. A change of bank details should be told to clients in advance, in a separate message, not only on the next invoice, and a client who receives a sudden change of bank details on an invoice alone should be expected to query it, because bank-detail fraud is a real risk, and a careful client is a good client. The guide to sending an invoice by email covers the covering message that should accompany the invoice, including the payment details and the reference to quote.

Tax and VAT Notes: General Terms Only

Tax is the part of sole trader invoicing where general guidance reaches its limit, and where a specific question must be answered by the rules of the jurisdiction and, where needed, by a qualified adviser. This section therefore states the questions to ask and the notes to show, in general terms, and does not state the tax position for any country. The questions are: Are you registered for the relevant tax, such as value added tax or a sales tax, in the jurisdiction where you are based and where the supply is made? If you are registered, what rate applies to the supply, and what registration number must be shown on the invoice? If you are not registered, must the invoice state that no tax has been charged, and in what words? What is the threshold for registration, and does your turnover require you to register? Does the tax treatment change if the client is in another country, if the supply is exported, or if the supply is of a particular type, such as a digital service? And what records must you keep, for how long, to support the tax treatment shown on the invoice? Each question has a different answer in different jurisdictions, and an answer copied from another trader, or from a guide written for another country, may be wrong for you.

On the face of the invoice, the tax treatment should be shown in a way that the client can check and that the tax authority can follow. A registered trader commonly shows the subtotal, the tax at the applicable rate, shown separately, with the rate and the registration number, and the total including tax. A trader who is not registered commonly shows a total with no tax line, and, where the local practice requires it, a short note stating that no tax has been charged, in the wording the local rules prescribe or that local practice accepts. A trader who charges tax at different rates for different items should show the rates item by item, so that the client can see which rate applies to which supply. A trader who supplies to a client in another country should show the tax treatment that applies to that supply, and should keep the evidence — the client's address, the nature of the supply, and the rule relied on — with the invoice. In every case, the tax line on the invoice must match the tax treatment in the quote, so that the client is not surprised by a total that differs from the price agreed. If the tax treatment changes — because the trader registers, deregisters, or crosses a threshold — tell clients in advance, apply the new treatment to new work from the stated date, and do not change the tax treatment retrospectively on work already quoted.

Three tax mistakes are common among sole traders, and each is avoidable. The first is charging tax without being registered, which misleads the client and creates a liability for tax that was never due. The second is failing to charge tax when registered, which leaves the trader to pay the tax out of the invoiced total, and creates a shortfall. The third is showing a tax line with no registration number, no rate, and no explanation, which the client cannot check and the tax authority cannot follow. Each mistake is avoided by the same habit: check the registration position before the first invoice, state it on the invoice in the form the local rules require, review it when turnover or the type of work changes, and ask a qualified adviser when the position is unclear. The cost of an hour of advice before the first invoice is far smaller than the cost of correcting a year's invoices afterwards. This guide is general information only, and it is not a substitute for that advice.

Payment Terms for a Sole Trader

Payment terms are the part of the invoice that decides when, and how reliably, the trader is paid, and a sole trader, who has no separate cash reserve to absorb a late payment, should set them deliberately. The terms to state, in writing, before the work starts and again on the invoice, are: the date by which payment is due, commonly expressed as a number of days from the invoice date, or as a fixed date; the method of payment, with the bank or payment details shown accurately; the currency; any deposit or instalment arrangement, including a deposit before work starts and a balance on completion, where that is the agreement; and the consequence of late payment, where the jurisdiction allows a late-payment charge or interest, stated in the terms agreed with the client and in accordance with local law. Our payment terms guide covers each of those points in detail, and our late payment guide covers the steps to take when payment is late. The sole trader's version of the same advice is shorter: state the terms before the work starts, state them again on the invoice, and enforce them politely and consistently, because a term that is never enforced is not a term, it is a hope.

Deposits and instalments deserve a particular mention for sole traders, because they protect the trader's cash flow and the client's confidence at the same time. A deposit before work starts, commonly a proportion of the fixed price, shows that the client is committed, and it gives the trader funds to begin the work. The deposit invoice, or the proforma invoice where that document is appropriate, should refer to the quote, state that it is a deposit, and state the balance that will fall due on completion. The final invoice should then show the fixed price, less the deposit already received, credited on the face of the invoice, plus any agreed extras, equals the balance due, so that the client can see how the balance was reached. A final invoice that shows only the balance, without the fixed price and the deposit credit, will be queried, because the client cannot see the calculation. The same principle applies to instalments at milestones: each instalment invoice should show the milestone, the amount, the total price, the amounts already invoiced, and the balance remaining. The invoice, at every stage, should explain itself.

When payment is late, the sole trader's course is the same as any trader's: a polite reminder, referring to the invoice number, the amount, and the due date, with the invoice attached again, followed, if needed, by a firmer reminder stating the terms agreed, and then by the steps in the late payment guide. Keep a record of every reminder, with dates, because a record of polite, prompt reminders is the evidence that the trader acted reasonably, and it is the record that will be needed if the matter escalates. Do not let a late payment drift because the client is also a friend, or because the amount is small, because a small late payment that is not chased teaches the client that the trader's terms do not matter, and the next payment will be later still. Chase every invoice, politely, on time, and keep the relationship and the payment separate: the reminder can be warm in tone and firm in content, and the two are not in conflict.

Record-Keeping: The File Behind Every Invoice

Record-keeping is the part of sole trader invoicing that is most often neglected, and it is the part that matters most when a query, a tax return, or an audit arrives. For each job, keep a file, paper or electronic, containing the quote or written confirmation, the client's acceptance, any change agreed in writing, the invoice or invoices, any credit note, the payment record showing the date and amount received and the method of payment, and any correspondence about the job that affects the price or the scope. Keep the files in invoice-number order, or in client order with an index by invoice number, so that any invoice can be found in a minute. Keep a register or a spreadsheet that lists, for each invoice, the number, the date, the client, the amount, the tax treatment, the due date, and the date paid, so that the state of the business — what is owed, what is overdue, and what has been paid — can be seen at a glance. The register is the trader's control panel, and a trader who cannot see, at a glance, what is owed, cannot manage cash flow.

Keep the records for the period your jurisdiction requires, which is commonly several years, and check the exact period where you are based rather than relying on a general figure. Keep them in a form that can be produced on request: electronic copies should be legible, backed up, and stored in a place that will survive a computer failure, and paper copies should be filed, not piled. Keep the records for income and for expenses together, because a tax return needs both, and keep the bank statements that show the payments received, so that the income can be reconciled to the bank. If the records are kept electronically, use a consistent naming convention — invoice number, client, date — so that a file can be found without opening it. If a record is corrected, keep the original and the correction, with the reason noted, rather than overwriting the original, because a corrected record that hides the original is a record that cannot be trusted. The habit to build is simple: file it the day it is created, back it up the day it is filed, and review the register weekly, so that overdue invoices are chased promptly and the business is never run from memory.

Record-keeping also includes the trader's own time and costs, where those affect pricing. Keep a note of the time spent on each job, even for project-priced work, so that the next similar job can be priced from evidence rather than from a guess, and keep the receipts for business expenses, filed by date, with a note of the job or the purpose they relate to. A sole trader who knows, from records, how long a job takes and what it costs, can price the next job with confidence. A sole trader who guesses will underprice, overwork, and wonder why the business is busy but not profitable. The invoice is the visible end of the record-keeping habit. The file behind it is the habit itself, and the file is what makes the invoice defensible, years later, when the job itself is long forgotten.

Worked Example: A Sole Trader Invoice, Labelled Illustrative

Every figure in this section is illustrative and invented for the example only. The names, rates, hours, and prices are made up, to show the structure of a sole trader invoice. Replace every figure with your own, keep the structure, and check the calculation and the tax treatment before you send a quote or an invoice.

Illustrative example. A sole trader, trading as "Illustrative Design Studio", whose own name is shown in the identity block, agrees a fixed price for a defined deliverable: a logo with three concepts and two revision rounds, for an illustrative fixed price of 900.00 in the local currency, with a deposit of 50% before work starts and the balance on delivery, and with payment due within 14 days of each invoice. The trader is not registered for the relevant tax in this invented example, so no tax is shown, and the invoice carries a short note, in the wording the trader's jurisdiction requires, stating that no tax has been charged. The quote, numbered as a quote reference, lists the deliverables, the exclusions, and the payment terms, and the client accepts it in writing. The trader issues a deposit invoice, numbered INV-0001, dated the day the work is agreed, showing the client, the description "Deposit — 50% of fixed price for logo design, quote reference Q-0001", the amount 450.00, the total due 450.00, and the payment terms. The client pays the deposit. On delivery, the trader issues the final invoice, numbered INV-0002, showing the fixed price of 900.00, less the deposit of 450.00 already received, credited on the face of the invoice, plus an agreed extra revision round, agreed in writing before the work was done, at an illustrative price of 100.00, giving a balance due of 550.00. The final invoice shows the whole calculation, the quote reference, the deposit invoice number, and the payment terms, so that the client can check it without asking. Both invoices, the quote, the acceptance, the change agreement, and the payment records are filed together, under the client, in invoice-number order, and the register is updated to show INV-0001 paid and INV-0002 due within 14 days.

The example shows the four habits that matter: the agreement in writing before the work, the deposit credited on the face of the final invoice, the agreed extra shown separately, and the file kept together. It also shows what the example does not show: the tax position, which is invented as "not registered" for the example, and which must be checked for the real trader, in the real jurisdiction, before any invoice is sent. A real sole trader who copies the example's tax treatment without checking it will copy a fiction. A real sole trader who copies the example's structure — quote, deposit, final invoice with the deposit credited, agreed extra shown separately, file kept together — will have an invoice that a client can check, a record that a tax authority can follow, and a business that can be managed from its records rather than from memory.

Common Mistakes Sole Traders Make When Invoicing

Reusing or skipping invoice numbers. One sequence, no gaps, no repeats, with voided numbers kept in the file marked void. Changing the trading name without telling clients. Change a name cleanly, from a stated invoice number, with clients told in advance, and a note in the file. Describing the supply as only "services". Name the deliverable, the date or period, and the basis of the charge, so the invoice can be checked and understood later. Copying another trader's tax treatment. Check your own registration position, show the tax treatment the local rules require, and ask a qualified adviser when unsure. Forgetting the deposit credit. A deposit already received must be credited on the final invoice, in plain figures. Charging the full price again is an error the client will notice. Stating no payment terms. State the due date, the method, and the currency on the quote and again on the invoice, and enforce the terms politely and consistently. Issuing an invoice with no written agreement behind it. Get the quote or confirmation accepted in writing before the work starts, and invoice from it. Keeping no file. File the quote, the acceptance, the invoice, the payment record, and any change agreement together, per job, and back the file up. Not chasing late payment. Chase every invoice, politely, on time, with the invoice attached again, and keep a record of every reminder. Mixing business and personal money with no record. Keep the payment record for each invoice, reconcile it to the bank, and keep the register up to date, so that income can be proved and cash flow can be managed.

A Sole Trader Invoicing Checklist

Use the checklist below, in order, for every job, until it becomes a habit. First, agree the work in writing: the deliverables, the price or rate, the tax treatment, and the payment terms, in a quote or a written confirmation, and get the client's acceptance in writing. Second, check the identity block: the name that will appear on the invoice, the name on the bank account, and the address and contact details, used consistently on the quote and the invoice. Third, check the tax position: registration, rate, and the wording and number to show, with a qualified adviser consulted where the position is unclear. Fourth, issue the invoice, with the next number in the sequence, the date, the client details, a clear description, checkable amounts, and the payment terms, using the free invoice generator on this site to produce the invoice in a consistent layout. Fifth, send the invoice by the method the client prefers, with a short covering message, as explained in our guide to sending an invoice by email. Sixth, file the quote, the acceptance, the invoice, and any change agreement together, update the register, and set a reminder for the due date. Seventh, chase promptly if payment is late, politely, with the invoice attached again. Eighth, record the payment, with the date, the amount, and the method, and reconcile it to the bank. Ninth, review the register weekly, so that overdue invoices, unfiled documents, and gaps in the sequence are caught at once. Frequently asked questions

What must a sole trader invoice include?

A sole trader invoice should show, at minimum, a unique invoice number, the invoice date, your name and contact details, the client's name and address, a clear description of the goods or services supplied, the quantities and prices, the subtotal, any tax shown separately where it applies, the total due, and the payment terms including the due date and the method of payment. Where a tax or VAT registration applies, the relevant registration number and the tax treatment of the supply should also be shown. The exact requirements vary by jurisdiction, so check the rules where you are based, and ask a qualified adviser about any field you are unsure of.

Should a sole trader invoice in a business name or a personal name?

A sole trader is the business, in the sense that there is no separate legal person between the trader and the business, so the invoice must identify the trader clearly enough for the client and for the tax authority to know who supplied the goods or services and who is to be paid. Many sole traders trade under a business name and show that name prominently, with their own name shown as well where that is required or where it avoids confusion. Show the name that appears on your bank account for payment, so the client pays the right person, and use the same name consistently on quotes, invoices, and receipts. Check the local rules on trading names and on whether your own name must appear, because the position varies by jurisdiction.

Does a sole trader need to charge VAT or sales tax on an invoice?

Whether a sole trader must charge tax depends on the jurisdiction, on whether the trader is registered for the relevant tax, on the type of goods or services supplied, and on the level of turnover, and the answer cannot be given in general terms for every country. A trader who is registered commonly shows the tax separately on the invoice, states the rate applied, and shows a registration number, while a trader who is not registered commonly states, where that is the correct position, that no tax has been charged. Do not copy a tax treatment from another trader's invoice. Check the registration and invoicing rules where you are based, and ask a qualified tax adviser before you charge or stop charging tax, because an invoice that charges tax incorrectly, or that omits tax that should have been charged, can create a problem for both you and the client.

How long should a sole trader keep invoice records?

Keep every invoice you issue and receive, with the quote, the acceptance, the payment record, and any credit note, for the period your jurisdiction requires for tax and accounting records, which is commonly several years. The exact period varies, so check the rule where you are based rather than relying on a general figure. Keep the records in a form that can be produced on request, with the invoice numbers in one unbroken sequence, and keep a backup of electronic records. Good record-keeping is not only a legal requirement; it is also the evidence that answers a client's query, supports a tax return, and proves, years later, what was agreed and what was paid.
