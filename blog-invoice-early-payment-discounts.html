Early-Payment Discounts: Offer Them Without Losing Money

Updated 28 November 2026 · 16-minute read

Quick answer: An early-payment discount is a small, stated reduction in the amount payable if the client pays by an earlier date than the standard due date — for example, 2 percent off if paid within 10 days, with the full amount due in 30 days. It can help cash flow, but it costs margin, and it only helps where the client would otherwise pay slowly. Work out what the discount costs on a typical invoice, compare it with the value of being paid sooner, offer it only where the comparison favours it, state it plainly on the invoice before the client pays, and keep the record of what was agreed, what was invoiced, and what arrived. This guide explains the maths, the choice of rate, the wording, and the alternatives, using illustrative figures that are invented and labelled as such. It is general information only, not legal or tax advice. Every freelancer knows the gap between finishing work and being paid for it. The work is done, the invoice is sent, and then the money arrives, or does not arrive, on a timetable the freelancer does not control. A client who pays in thirty days is not late, under thirty-day terms, and yet the freelancer has waited a month for money that was earned in the first week, has paid the costs of the month from savings or from other work, and has spent time checking, reminding, and wondering whether the payment is on its way. An early-payment discount is one answer to that gap: a small reward, offered in advance, for paying sooner. It is a simple idea, and it is easy to get wrong, because the reward is certain — the client who takes it pays less — while the benefit is uncertain — the client may have paid promptly anyway, the earlier payment may be worth less than the discount, and the discount, once offered, may become the price the client expects on every invoice. This guide treats the discount as a commercial decision, to be made from figures, stated in advance, and reviewed after a few invoices, rather than as a habit, a gesture, or a plea for prompt payment dressed up as a discount.

The most useful way to think about an early-payment discount is as buying time with margin. The freelancer gives up a small part of the invoice amount, and in return receives the rest of the amount sooner. Whether that trade is worth making depends on three things: how much margin the work carries, how much sooner the money actually arrives, and what the freelancer would otherwise pay — in borrowing, in chasing, in delayed spending, or in forgone work — for waiting. A discount that buys three weeks, on work with a healthy margin, for a client who would otherwise pay at the last possible day, may be a good trade. A discount that buys three days, on work with a thin margin, for a client who already pays in a week, is a poor trade, and the freelancer would have been better served by shorter terms, a deposit, or no discount at all. The sections that follow work through that comparison in order: first what the discount is and how it is written, then why a freelancer might offer one, then the maths, then the choice of rate, then who it suits, then the wording, the record, and the alternatives, so that the decision, when it is made, is a decision rather than a hope.

1. What an Early-Payment Discount Is, and How It Is Written

An early-payment discount is a reduction in the amount payable, offered in advance, if the client pays by a date earlier than the standard due date. It is commonly written as a rate, an early date, and a standard date, in a short form borrowed from business practice: "2/10, net 30" means 2 percent off if paid within 10 days, with the full amount due in 30 days. The short form is useful as shorthand, but it should not be the only wording on an invoice, because many clients, and many freelancers, will not know what it means, and because a discount that depends on a code the client does not understand will produce queries rather than prompt payment. Write it in full, in plain words, on the invoice: the full amount, the discount rate, the date by which payment must be received for the discount to apply, the discounted amount, and the standard due date for the full amount. A client who can see, at a glance, that paying by one date costs less than paying by another, and by how much, is a client who can act on the offer without writing to ask what it means.

Four features distinguish an early-payment discount from the other payment devices a freelancer uses, and each of them matters for how the discount is agreed and recorded. First, it is voluntary: the client may take it or not, by paying early or by paying on the standard date, and the freelancer cannot require the client to pay early. Second, it is agreed in advance: it is stated in the quote or agreement and on the invoice, before the client pays, not offered after the event, not negotiated after the invoice is sent, and not applied retrospectively to an invoice that has already been paid. Third, it is a reduction in the amount payable, not a refund, not a credit note issued later, and not a change to the price of the work: the price is the full amount, and the discount is the amount forgone for early payment, recorded as such. Fourth, it is separate from a late-payment charge: the discount rewards early payment, a late charge penalises late payment, and the two should not be confused, combined, or presented as if one were the other. A freelancer who wants both — a discount for early payment and a charge for late payment — should state both, separately, in the payment terms, and should check the rules that apply where the freelancer is based before stating either, because the enforceability and the tax treatment of both vary by jurisdiction. Our guide to payment terms explains the terms themselves, and our guide to late payment explains the chasing sequence; this guide covers only the discount.

The discount also differs from the other common ways of getting paid sooner, and the difference should be kept in mind when choosing between them. A deposit is money paid before or at the start of the work, and it reduces the amount outstanding from the beginning. Staged payments are amounts paid at agreed points during the work, and they spread the freelancer's risk across the project. Shorter payment terms — fourteen days instead of thirty, or seven days instead of fourteen — require the full amount sooner, without giving anything up. An early-payment discount leaves the full amount outstanding, offers a reduction for paying it sooner, and costs the freelancer the discount if the client takes it. Each device suits a different situation, and a freelancer may use several of them for different clients, or for different projects with the same client, provided each is stated plainly and each is recorded. The discount is the right device where the freelancer wants to encourage, rather than require, earlier payment, and where the cost of the encouragement is less than the value of the earlier payment. Section 10 compares the devices directly, so that the choice is made deliberately.

2. Why Offer a Discount for Paying Early?

There are four honest reasons to offer an early-payment discount, and each of them should be tested against the freelancer's own figures before the offer is made. The first is cash flow. A freelancer who is paid sooner can pay the costs of the month — rent, software, subcontractors, materials — from the income of the work, rather than from savings or from borrowing, and can take on new work without waiting for old work to be paid. The value of that is not abstract: it is the interest, the fees, or the stress of the gap between earning and receiving, and it is real even where no borrowing is involved, because money received sooner is money that can be used sooner. The second reason is the cost of chasing. A client who pays early does not need to be reminded, and a freelancer who does not need to remind saves the time of the reminder, the awkwardness of the chase, and the risk that a reminder, however polite, strains the relationship. The third reason is certainty. A payment received is a fact; a payment promised for a future date is a hope, and a freelancer who has been promised payment, and has waited, and has been promised again, knows the difference. A discount that converts a hope into a fact, sooner, has a value beyond the amount of the discount, because it removes a month of uncertainty. The fourth reason is the relationship. A client who is offered a genuine, stated benefit for paying promptly, and who takes it, has a reason to pay promptly again, and the discount, used sparingly, can become part of a working relationship in which prompt payment is the norm, rather than the exception.

There are also reasons not to offer a discount, and they should be weighed with the same honesty. The first is that the discount costs margin, directly and certainly, on every invoice on which it is taken, and a freelancer whose margins are thin, whose prices are already low, or whose work carries significant costs that must be paid regardless of when the client pays, may not be able to carry the cost. The second is that the discount may buy nothing: a client who already pays promptly, who pays by an automated system on a fixed date, or who pays early because the client's own process requires it, will take the discount without changing behaviour, and the freelancer will have given away margin for a payment that would have arrived early anyway. The third is that the discount may become an expectation: a client who receives a discount on one invoice will expect it on the next, and on every invoice thereafter, and will treat the discounted amount, not the full amount, as the price, so that removing the discount feels like a price increase, even though the price has not changed. The fourth is that the discount may attract the wrong attention: a client who is offered a discount for paying early may infer that the freelancer is short of cash, and may use that inference in a negotiation about price, or about terms, on the next project. None of these reasons rules out the discount. Each of them rules out offering it without thought, to every client, on every invoice, as a habit.

The test, in practice, is a simple one: offer the discount where it solves a problem the freelancer actually has, for a client whose behaviour it is likely to change, at a cost the margin can carry, and review it after a few invoices. If the client pays earlier, and the earlier payment is worth more than the discount, keep the offer. If the client pays at the same time as before, and takes the discount, withdraw the offer for that client, politely, and use a different device — shorter terms, a deposit, or staged payments — instead. If the discount is taken by every client, and the total cost, over a quarter, is larger than the benefit, withdraw it generally, and keep it only for the clients and the invoices where it demonstrably helps. A discount is a tool, not a policy, and a tool that is not reviewed will drift into a habit, and a habit will drift into a cost that no one remembers agreeing to.

3. The Maths: What a Discount Actually Costs

The maths of an early-payment discount is simple, and it should be done before the offer is made, on the freelancer's own typical figures, rather than after the offer has been taken. The direct cost is the rate multiplied by the invoice amount: a 2 percent discount on an invoice of 1,000 units of currency costs 20 units, and the freelancer receives 980. A 1 percent discount on the same invoice costs 10, and the freelancer receives 990. A 5 percent discount costs 50, and the freelancer receives 950. Those are the direct costs, and they are the costs the freelancer will see, in the bank, on every invoice on which the discount is taken. The indirect question is what the earlier payment is worth, and that question has no single answer, because it depends on what the freelancer would otherwise do while waiting: borrow, draw on savings, delay spending, or forgo work. A useful way to think about it, without pretending to a precision the figures do not support, is to ask what a month of waiting costs, in the freelancer's own experience, and to compare that cost, honestly, with the discount. If waiting a month commonly costs more, in interest, fees, chasing time, or lost opportunity, than the discount, the discount is worth offering. If waiting costs less, it is not.

A second calculation, which freelancers often miss, is the annualised cost of the discount, which shows how expensive a small rate can be when it is repeated. A 2 percent discount for paying twenty days early — ten days instead of thirty — is not a 2 percent cost. It is 2 percent for twenty days, repeated, in principle, over the year, and the annualised rate, on a simple calculation, is many times the headline rate. The point of that calculation is not to frighten the freelancer away from offering a discount. It is to prevent the freelancer from thinking of 2 percent as "only 2 percent", when the same 2 percent, taken by every client, on every invoice, over a year, is a substantial reduction in annual income, for a benefit — earlier payment — that may be worth much less. The calculation should be done on the freelancer's own figures, for the freelancer's own typical invoice and typical payment pattern, and it should be done before the offer, not after a quarter of discounts has accumulated. A discount that looks small on one invoice can look large on a year's income, and a freelancer who has not done the annual calculation will not know, until the year is over, how much the discount cost.

A third calculation is the break-even: how much sooner must the payment arrive, for the discount to be worth it, given the freelancer's own cost of waiting. If the freelancer's cost of waiting is, in effect, the cost of borrowing, or the return forgone on savings, the break-even can be estimated, but the estimate will be rough, and it should be treated as rough. The more useful break-even, for most freelancers, is a practical one: the discount is worth it if it moves the payment from a date that causes a problem — a rent date, a subcontractor payment, a tax date — to a date that does not, and it is not worth it if the payment, early or late, arrives comfortably before any problem arises. A freelancer who is paid comfortably, within terms, by most clients, and who has a reserve for the gap, gains little from a discount, and should not offer one. A freelancer who is regularly caught short, who chases, who borrows, or who delays spending while waiting, gains more, and should weigh the discount against the alternatives in section 10, rather than against doing nothing. The maths, in the end, is a comparison of two costs: the cost of the discount, which is certain, and the cost of waiting, which is real but harder to measure. Do the certain calculation first, estimate the uncertain one honestly, and offer the discount only where the certain cost is the smaller of the two.

4. Worked Example: One Invoice, Three Ways (Illustrative Figures)

The example below is invented, with invented figures, labelled as such, to show the shape of the decision, not to state what any real invoice will cost. The imaginary freelancer has invoiced a client 1,000 units for a project, on thirty-day terms, and the work carries costs of 400 units that must be paid regardless of when the client pays, leaving a margin of 600 units before the freelancer's own time and overheads. Three ways of being paid are compared: payment on day 30 with no discount, payment on day 10 with a 2 percent discount, and payment on day 10 with a 1 percent discount. The figures are simple, and they are chosen to make the comparison clear, not to represent any particular freelancer's position.

Payment on day 30, no discount. The freelancer receives 1,000 units, thirty days after the invoice. The margin, before the freelancer's own time and overheads, is 600 units. The cost of waiting is the cost the freelancer bears during those thirty days: the costs of 400 units must be carried, the freelancer's own spending must be carried, and the payment must be checked and, if it is late, chased. In the example, that cost is not given a figure, because it varies, but the freelancer is asked to supply it from experience: what did the last three months of waiting actually cost, in interest, fees, chasing time, or delayed work. If the answer is "very little", because the freelancer has a reserve and the clients pay on time, the discount has little to buy, and the comparison ends here, in favour of no discount.

Payment on day 10, 2 percent discount. The freelancer receives 980 units, ten days after the invoice. The margin, before the freelancer's own time and overheads, is 580 units: 20 units less than the no-discount case. The benefit is twenty days sooner, and the removal of twenty days of waiting, checking, and chasing. The question is whether twenty days sooner is worth 20 units. If the freelancer would otherwise borrow, or pay a fee, or delay a payment that carries a charge, and if that cost, over twenty days, is more than 20 units, the discount is worth it. If the cost of waiting is less than 20 units, or if the client would have paid on day 10 anyway, without the discount, the discount is a loss of 20 units for no benefit, and it should not have been offered to that client.

Payment on day 10, 1 percent discount. The freelancer receives 990 units, ten days after the invoice. The margin is 590 units: 10 units less than the no-discount case, and 10 units more than the 2 percent case. The benefit is the same twenty days sooner, for half the cost. For many freelancers, 1 percent is the more sensible starting rate, because it tests whether the client responds to a small incentive, at a small cost, before a larger rate is offered. If the client pays early for 1 percent, the freelancer has learned that the client responds, and the rate need not be raised. If the client does not pay early for 1 percent, the freelancer has learned that a small incentive does not move that client, and should consider whether a larger rate would move them, or whether a different device — shorter terms, a deposit, or staged payments — would serve better. The example is invented, and no real invoice will follow it exactly, but its shape — full amount, discount cost, margin after discount, benefit of earlier payment, and the comparison of the two — is the shape every real decision should follow, on the freelancer's own figures, before the offer is made.

Two further points should be noted from the example, because they are often missed. The first is that the discount is a cost on the invoice on which it is taken, not a cost spread over the year, and a freelancer who offers 2 percent to every client, on every invoice, will see 2 percent less income, in total, over the year, unless the earlier payments produce a benefit that exceeds that 2 percent. The second is that the discount, once taken, is gone: the freelancer cannot recover it by charging more on the next invoice, without the client noticing that the price has risen, and the client who has grown used to the discount will treat the full amount as a price increase. The discount should therefore be offered as a stated, time-limited, reviewable arrangement, not as a permanent feature of the freelancer's terms, and it should be reviewed, as section 2 advises, after a few invoices, with the total cost and the total benefit both recorded.

5. Choosing a Rate and Terms That Do Not Lose Money

The rate should be chosen from the margin, not from habit, and not from what another freelancer offers. A freelancer whose work carries a margin of, say, half the invoice amount can carry a 2 percent discount more easily than a freelancer whose margin is a tenth of the invoice amount, because the same 2 percent is a smaller proportion of the margin in the first case than in the second. Work out the margin on a typical invoice, in the freelancer's own figures, subtract the discount at the rate being considered, and ask whether the remaining margin is still enough to cover the freelancer's own time, overheads, and the risk of the work. If it is not, the rate is too high, however common it may be, and a lower rate, or no discount, is the right choice. A rate that is chosen without reference to the margin is a rate that may lose money on every invoice on which it is taken, and the freelancer will not know, until the year is over, because the loss is small on each invoice and large in total.

The early period should be chosen to match the benefit being bought. A discount for payment within 7 days, on thirty-day terms, buys twenty-three days sooner. A discount for payment within 10 days buys twenty days sooner. A discount for payment within 20 days, on thirty-day terms, buys only ten days sooner, for the same cost, and is a poor trade unless the freelancer's problem is specifically the last ten days of the term. The early period should therefore be short enough to buy a meaningful difference, and long enough for the client to act: a client who pays by a monthly payment run, or by a finance department that processes invoices weekly, cannot pay within three days, however willing, and a discount that requires the impossible will not be taken, and will produce a query rather than a payment. Ask, where possible, how the client pays, and set the early date to a date the client's process can meet. A discount that is taken is a discount that worked. A discount that is never taken, because the date is impossible, is a discount that cost nothing and bought nothing, and that should be reset to a date the client can meet, or withdrawn.

The standard terms should remain the standard terms, and the discount should not be allowed to become the terms. The invoice should state the full amount, the standard due date, and the discount as a separate, conditional reduction, so that a client who pays after the early date, but within the standard term, pays the full amount, without argument, and without feeling that the full amount is a penalty. The discount should also be time-limited in the freelancer's own planning, even if it is not time-limited on the invoice: review it after a set number of invoices, or after a set period, and decide, from the record, whether to keep it, change the rate, change the early date, or withdraw it. A discount that is reviewed is a tool. A discount that is never reviewed is a permanent reduction in price, by another name, and it should be recognised as such before it is offered, not after it has become the price.

6. Who the Discount Suits, and Who It Does Not

The discount suits a client who pays slowly, but not very slowly; who pays, eventually, in full; and whose slowness is a matter of process, priority, or cash flow, rather than of dispute or inability to pay. A client who pays on day 29, every time, because the client's process runs monthly, is a client a discount may move, if the early date is set to a date the client's process can meet, or if the discount gives the client's finance contact a reason to prioritise the invoice. A client who pays on day 45, on thirty-day terms, because the client is disorganised, may also be moved, if the discount is large enough and the early date is clear. A client who does not pay at all, who disputes the work, or who cannot pay, will not be moved by a discount, and offering one to such a client is a waste of margin and a distraction from the real problem, which is the dispute, the inability, or the need for a different payment arrangement, such as payment in advance. Our guide to late payment covers the chasing sequence for clients who pay late; this guide does not repeat it, and a discount should not be offered as a substitute for chasing a genuinely overdue invoice.

The discount suits a freelancer whose margin can carry it, whose cash flow is genuinely helped by earlier payment, and who can keep the record that the discount requires. It does not suit a freelancer whose margin is thin, whose prices are already at the lowest the freelancer can accept, or who would need to raise prices to carry the discount, because a price rise to fund a discount is a price rise by another name, and the client will see it as such. It does not suit work where the costs are high and must be paid upfront, and where the discount would reduce the amount available to pay them, unless the earlier payment is itself the means of paying them, and the timing works. It does not suit a new client relationship where the freelancer does not yet know whether the client pays promptly, because the discount, offered before the pattern is known, may be given away for a payment that would have arrived early anyway. For a new client, the safer course is to set clear terms, to require a deposit or staged payments where the risk justifies it, and to observe the payment pattern for a few invoices before offering a discount. The discount is a reward for a pattern, not a guess about one.

The discount also suits some kinds of work better than others. It suits repeat work, for a regular client, where the discount can become part of a working rhythm, and where the freelancer can judge, over several invoices, whether it helps. It suits large invoices, where the amount outstanding is large enough that earlier payment matters, and where the discount, though larger in absolute terms, is a small proportion of a large amount. It suits work where the freelancer's costs are low and the margin is healthy, and where the earlier payment can be used productively. It suits less well one-off work, where there is no pattern to judge and no relationship to build, small invoices, where the discount is a trivial amount to the client and may not motivate, and work where the client is a large organisation with a fixed payment cycle, where no discount, however large, will move the payment date, because the date is set by a system, not by a person. A freelancer who offers a discount to a large organisation should ask, first, whether the organisation can pay early at all, because a discount offered to a client who cannot take it is a discount that only produces a query.

7. Wording It: On the Quote, On the Invoice, and in the Agreement

The discount should be stated in the same words, in the quote or agreement and on the invoice, so that there is no difference between what was agreed and what is invoiced, and no room for a dispute about which document governs. State, in plain words, the full amount, the discount rate, the early date by which payment must be received, the discounted amount payable by that date, and the standard due date for the full amount. State whether the early date is the date payment is sent or the date it is received, because a payment sent on the early date and received three days later is a common source of dispute, and the invoice should settle it in advance: in most cases, the date of receipt, as shown by the bank or payment record, is the fair and checkable date, and it should be stated as such. State the currency on every amount, as our guide to international clients explains, and state the payment method, so that the client knows how to pay early, not only that early payment is rewarded.

Wording that can be adapted, for a thirty-day invoice with a ten-day early period, is as follows: "Total due: 1,000 units, due 30 days from the invoice date. Early-payment discount: 2 percent (20 units) if payment is received within 10 days of the invoice date, making 980 units payable by [early date]. If payment is received after [early date], the full amount of 1,000 units is due by [standard due date]." The bracketed dates should be filled in with actual dates, not left as periods, because a client who must calculate the date may calculate it wrongly, and a dispute about a date that was never stated is a dispute that need not have happened. The wording should appear on the invoice itself, not only in an email, because the invoice is the document the client's finance contact will see, and the email may not reach that contact. The quote or agreement should carry the same wording, so that the client who accepted the quote has already accepted the discount terms, and the invoice contains no surprise.

Three wording cautions should be observed. First, do not present the discount as a penalty for paying late, or the full amount as a surcharge: the full amount is the price, the discount is a reduction for early payment, and the wording should say so, because a client who feels penalised for paying on time, within terms, will resent the arrangement, however fairly it is drawn. Second, do not state the discount in a way that suggests it applies to part of the invoice, to a future invoice, or to work not yet done, unless that is the intention, and unless it is stated plainly: a discount on one invoice should be a discount on that invoice, and a discount on future work should be a separate agreement. Third, do not offer the discount verbally, without writing it down, because a verbal discount, taken or not taken, will be remembered differently by the two sides, and the record — the quote, the invoice, and the payment — should agree. If the discount is agreed in a call or a meeting, write it down afterwards, in an email or a revised quote, and keep the written version with the invoice. A discount that is not written down is a discount that will be disputed.

8. Discounts and the Other Payment Devices: Keeping Them Separate

A freelancer commonly uses several payment devices at once — payment terms, late charges, deposits, staged payments, recurring billing, proforma invoices, and, for some clients, an early-payment discount — and the devices should be kept separate, in the terms and on the invoice, so that each does its own job and none is confused with another. The payment terms state when the full amount is due. The late-payment provisions state what happens if it is not paid by then. The discount states what happens if it is paid early. The deposit states what is paid before the work. The staged payments state what is paid at each stage. Each should be stated in its own line, in its own words, and the invoice should show the arithmetic plainly, so that the client, and the freelancer's own record, can follow it without a calculation. Our guides to payment terms, to late payment, to recurring invoices, and to proforma invoices explain each device in depth, and this guide does not repeat them. The point here is the separation: a discount that is mixed into the terms, or into a late charge, or into a deposit, will be misunderstood, and a misunderstood discount will be disputed.

The interaction with recurring invoices deserves a specific note, because a discount on a recurring invoice can become, very quickly, a permanent reduction in the recurring price. A client who is invoiced monthly, on the same terms, and who takes an early-payment discount every month, will, over a year, pay the discounted amount every month, and will treat that amount as the price. If the freelancer then withdraws the discount, the client will experience a price rise, even though the price has not changed. The safer course for recurring work is to set the recurring terms — the amount, the interval, and the due date — without a discount, and to offer a discount only for a specific reason, for a specific period, stated as such, or to build the prompt-payment expectation into the terms themselves, by setting a due date the client can meet and by chasing promptly when it is missed. Our guide to recurring invoices explains the recurring pattern; the discount, where it is used for recurring work, should be reviewed more often, not less, because its cost accumulates invisibly, month by month.

The interaction with international invoicing also deserves a note, because an early-payment discount on an international invoice carries the same complications as any cross-border payment: the currency, the exchange rate, the charges, and the time the payment takes to arrive. A discount that depends on the date of receipt must allow for the transit time of an international payment, which may be several working days, and for time zones, bank holidays, and compliance checks in the client's country, which may delay receipt without any fault on the client's part. The early date should therefore be set with the transit time in mind, and the wording should state that the date is the date of receipt, as shown by the bank or provider record. The currency should be stated on every amount, including the discounted amount, and the charges should be agreed in advance, as our guide to international clients explains, so that a client who pays early, but through a route that deducts charges, is not treated as having paid less than the discounted amount, where the agreement said the client would bear the charges, or as having paid in full, where the agreement said otherwise. The discount does not remove the need for the international agreement. It adds to it, and the two should be agreed together, in writing, before the work.

9. Records and Tax Notes: General Terms Only

The record for an early-payment discount should be kept with the invoice, as one file, so that the agreement, the invoice, the payment, and the discount can all be checked from the same place, months later, without reconstruction. The file should contain the quote or agreement, with the discount terms as agreed; the invoice, with the full amount, the discount, the early date, the discounted amount, and the standard due date, all stated; the payment record, showing the amount received, the date received, and the method; and a note, where the discount was taken, of the discount amount and the basis on which it was allowed — the rate, the early date, and the date of receipt. Where the discount was not taken, the file should show the full amount received, on the standard date, without a note explaining why, because no explanation is needed. The record should be kept in the same way for every invoice, whether or not a discount was offered, so that the pattern — which clients take the discount, which pay early without one, which pay late — can be seen, over a quarter, from the records themselves, rather than from memory. A freelancer who cannot say, from the records, how much discounts cost over a quarter, and how much earlier the payments arrived, cannot judge whether the discount is working, and should not continue offering it until the record is complete.

The tax and accounting treatment of an early-payment discount is a matter for the rules where the freelancer is based, and for the type of supply, and it should not be guessed from a general guide. In general terms, the discount is a reduction in the amount received for the work, and it should be recorded as such — as a reduced receipt, not as a separate expense, and not as a price change after the event — but the precise treatment, including whether the tax charged on the invoice is charged on the full amount or on the discounted amount, and how the discount is shown in the freelancer's accounts, depends on the jurisdiction and on the freelancer's own accounting method. The freelancer should check the rules where the freelancer is based, and where the client is based, and should ask a qualified adviser about any specific invoice, tax treatment, or dispute, before charging tax, omitting tax, or copying a treatment from another invoice. This guide is general information only. It is not tax advice, and it is not legal advice, and no part of it should be treated as a statement of the law or of the tax rules for a particular invoice. The same caution applies to the interaction with late-payment charges, which are covered in general terms in our guide to late payment, and to the interaction with hourly versus project pricing, which is covered in our guide to hourly versus project pricing: the discount is a term of the invoice, and the tax and legal consequences of the term should be checked, not assumed.

The record should also include the review. After a set number of invoices, or after a set period — a quarter is a sensible period for most freelancers — the freelancer should total the discounts allowed, total the invoices on which they were allowed, note how much earlier, on average, the discounted payments arrived than the undiscounted payments, and judge whether the earlier arrival was worth the total cost. The review should be written down, in a sentence or two, with the date, so that the next review starts from the last one's conclusion, rather than from scratch. If the discount is working, keep it, and note the rate and the clients for whom it is kept. If it is not working, withdraw it, or change it, and note the change, so that the next offer is made from evidence, not from habit. A discount that is reviewed is a commercial decision. A discount that is never reviewed is a cost that no one owns, and it will continue, quietly, until the freelancer wonders why income is lower than expected, and cannot say where the difference went.

10. Alternatives That May Serve Better Than a Discount

A discount is one way to be paid sooner. It is not the only way, and for many freelancers it is not the best way, because it gives away margin for a benefit that may be small, uncertain, or available by another means, without cost. The alternatives below should be considered, for each client and each project, before a discount is offered, and the choice should be made deliberately, rather than by defaulting to the discount because it is the device most often suggested.

Shorter payment terms. Fourteen days instead of thirty, or seven days instead of fourteen, requires the full amount sooner, without giving anything up. Shorter terms suit clients who pay promptly anyway, clients who pay by an automated system, and work where the freelancer's costs are high and must be recovered quickly. They do not suit clients who cannot pay quickly, because their process will not allow it, and they should be agreed in the quote, not imposed on the invoice, because a term the client has not agreed is a term that will be queried. Our guide to payment terms explains how to set terms the client can meet.

A deposit, or payment in advance. A deposit — a proportion of the price, paid before or at the start of the work — reduces the amount outstanding from the beginning, and it tests the client's seriousness before the work is done. It suits new clients, large projects, and work where the freelancer must buy materials or book subcontractors. It should be stated in the quote, with the balance terms, and it should be recorded as part of the same file as the invoice. A deposit is not a discount, and it should not be presented as one: it is money paid early for the work, not a reduction in the price.

Staged payments. Payments at agreed stages — on acceptance, on delivery of a draft, on completion — spread the freelancer's risk and the client's outlay across the project, and they give both sides a rhythm of payment and delivery that a single invoice, at the end, does not. They suit long projects, projects with milestones, and clients who prefer to pay as work progresses. They should be agreed in advance, with the stages defined, and each stage should be invoiced separately, with its own due date, so that the record is clear.

Payment on delivery, or payment before delivery of final files. For some kinds of work — design files, documents, code — the freelancer can retain the final deliverable until payment is received, or until the balance is paid. That arrangement should be agreed in advance, in writing, and it should be stated plainly, because a client who expects delivery on completion, and who finds delivery withheld, will dispute the arrangement, however fair it may be. It suits work where the deliverable is the whole value, and where the freelancer has no other leverage if the client does not pay.

A prompt-payment expectation, without a discount. Clear terms, a clear due date, a polite reminder before the due date, and prompt chasing after it, will move many clients to pay on time, without any discount, because most late payment is disorganisation, not unwillingness, and a reminder, politely given, is often enough. Our guide to late payment explains the reminder sequence, and our guide to sending an invoice by email explains how to make the invoice itself easy to pay. A freelancer who has never tried the reminder sequence should try it before offering a discount, because the reminder costs nothing, and the discount costs margin.

The choice among these devices, and the discount, should be made per client, per project, and per pattern, and it should be reviewed. A client who pays promptly on short terms does not need a discount. A client who pays slowly, but reliably, may be helped by a discount, or by staged payments, or by a deposit, and the choice should follow the client's process, not the freelancer's preference. A client who does not pay at all needs a different response, which is not a discount, and which is covered in our guide to late payment. The freelancer who considers the alternatives, and who chooses deliberately, will offer fewer discounts, and will offer them where they help, which is the point of this guide: a discount offered where it helps is a tool, and a discount offered everywhere is a cost.

11. Checklist: Offering an Early-Payment Discount Without Losing Money

  1. Work out the cost. On a typical invoice, at the rate being considered, what does the discount cost, in money, and what proportion of the margin is it? Do the annual calculation as well: what would the discount cost, over a year, if every client took it?
  2. Work out the benefit. How much sooner would the payment arrive, for which clients, and what is that earlier arrival worth, in the freelancer's own experience of waiting, borrowing, chasing, or delaying spending?
  3. Choose the client. Offer the discount only where the client pays slowly, but reliably, and where the discount is likely to change the payment date. Do not offer it where the client already pays promptly, or where the client cannot pay early.
  4. Choose the rate and the dates. Set a rate the margin can carry, an early date the client's process can meet, and a standard due date that remains the standard. State whether the early date is the date of sending or of receipt.
  5. Agree it in writing. State the discount in the quote or agreement, in the same words as on the invoice, before the work starts or before the invoice is sent, so that there is no surprise.
  6. State it on the invoice. Show the full amount, the discount rate, the early date, the discounted amount, and the standard due date, all on the invoice, with the currency on every amount.
  7. Keep the record. Keep the agreement, the invoice, the payment record, and the discount note together, as one file, and record the discount as a reduction in the amount received.
  8. Check the tax and legal position. Check the rules where you are based, and ask a qualified adviser about any specific invoice, before charging or omitting tax, or copying a treatment from another invoice.
  9. Review it. After a set number of invoices, or after a quarter, total the discounts, total the benefit, and decide, from the record, whether to keep, change, or withdraw the offer. Write the decision down, with the date.
  10. Consider the alternatives first. Before offering a discount, consider shorter terms, a deposit, staged payments, or a prompt-payment routine, and offer the discount only where it serves better than those devices, for that client and that project.

An early-payment discount, offered in this order, is not a difficult device. It is an ordinary invoice, with a stated, conditional reduction, offered where the figures support it, stated plainly, recorded carefully, and reviewed regularly. The freelancer who works out the cost, who offers the discount only where it helps, who states it in the same words in the agreement and on the invoice, who keeps the record, and who reviews the result, will be paid sooner by the clients it helps, and will not lose money on the clients it does not help, because the discount will not be offered to them. The freelancer who offers a discount to every client, on every invoice, without working out the cost, without stating it plainly, and without reviewing it, will find that income is lower than expected, that the discount has become the price, and that removing it feels like a price increase, when the price has not changed. The choice is in the process, and the process is in the freelancer's control. Begin with the figures, and let the offer follow them. Frequently asked questions

What is an early-payment discount on an invoice?

An early-payment discount is a reduction in the amount payable if the client pays by an earlier date than the standard due date. It is commonly written as a rate and two dates, for example 2 percent off if paid within 10 days, with the full amount due in 30 days. The discount is voluntary, it must be agreed and stated on the invoice before the client pays, and the client who pays after the early date pays the full amount. It is not a late fee, and it is not a change to the price after the work is done.

What early-payment discount rate should a freelancer offer?

There is no single correct rate. A small rate, such as 1 or 2 percent, is common for a short early period, and the right rate for a particular freelancer depends on the margin in the work, the value of being paid sooner, and the cost of waiting or chasing payment. Work out, from the figures in section 3, what the discount costs on a typical invoice, and offer a rate only where the cost is less than the benefit of earlier payment. If the margin is thin, a shorter payment term, a deposit, or staged payments may be better than a discount, and those alternatives are covered in section 10.

Does an early-payment discount cost the freelancer money?

Yes, in the direct sense: a client who takes the discount pays less than the invoiced amount. Whether that cost is a loss depends on what earlier payment is worth. If being paid three weeks sooner avoids borrowing, avoids chasing, or lets the freelancer take on work that would otherwise wait, the discount may be worth less than the benefit. If the client would have paid promptly anyway, the discount is a gift of margin for no benefit. The worked example in section 4 shows how to compare the two, using illustrative figures, so that the decision is made from numbers rather than from a feeling about cash flow.

How do I show an early-payment discount on an invoice?

Show the full amount, the discount rate and the early date, the discounted amount payable by that date, and the standard due date for the full amount, all on the invoice, in plain words. State the discount before the client pays, in the quote or agreement as well as on the invoice, so that there is no surprise. Keep the agreement, the invoice, and the payment record together, and record the discount as a reduction in the amount received, not as a separate expense invented after the event. Section 7 gives wording that can be adapted, and the general tax and record-keeping notes are in section 9.

Should I offer an early-payment discount to every client?

No. Offer it where it solves a real problem: a client who pays slowly, a large invoice that would strain cash flow while it is outstanding, or a new relationship where prompt payment is worth encouraging. Do not offer it where the client already pays promptly, where the margin cannot carry it, or where the discount would become an expectation on every invoice, including invoices the client would have paid on time anyway. Section 6 sets out who the discount suits and who it does not, and section 10 sets out the alternatives for clients who do not need a discount to pay promptly.
