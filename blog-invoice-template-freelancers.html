Invoice Template for Freelancers

Updated 13 November 2026 · 7-minute read

Quick answer: A good freelance invoice template has your details, your client's details, a unique invoice number, the date and due date, clear line items, the total due and your payment details — nothing more, nothing less. Build it once, reuse it for every client, and you will spend minutes on billing instead of hours. The free invoice generator on this site turns the template below into a clean PDF in under a minute.

Most freelancers start invoicing with a document they threw together for the first client: a few lines of text, a total at the bottom, and a hope the client pays on time. It works until it does not — a client asks for your tax number, another asks what the payment is actually for, and a third pays the wrong amount because the invoice never said how to pay. A proper template fixes all three problems at once. It makes every invoice complete, consistent and easy to pay, and it protects you when a payment is late or disputed. This guide walks through every field a freelance invoice template needs, the payment terms that get you paid faster, and the mistakes that cost freelancers money.

What every freelance invoice template must include

Start with the basics. Your details go at the top: your full name or business name, your email, your phone number and your address. Clients file invoices, and an invoice without your contact details is hard to chase and hard to pay. Next, the client's details: the client's name, company name if there is one, and their billing address. If the client is a company, ask for the name of the person who approves payments — invoices addressed to a named person get paid faster than invoices addressed to a generic department. Then the invoice number: use a simple, unique sequence such as INV-001, INV-002, and never reuse a number. A unique number is what lets you and the client refer to the same invoice without confusion. Add the invoice date and the due date — the two dates clients check first. Finally, the line items: one row per service, with a clear description, the quantity or hours, the rate and the amount. "Logo design — 3 concepts, 2 revisions — $450" is a line item a client can approve without asking questions; "Design work — $450" is not.

Line items: describe the work the client recognises

The line items are where most freelance invoices fail. Vague descriptions invite questions, and questions delay payment. Write each line the way the client described the work in the project brief: "Landing page design", "Monthly SEO content — 4 articles", "Logo revision package". If the project was quoted as a fixed fee, show the agreed fee and the scope it covers. If it was hourly, show the hours and the rate, so the client can check the maths. For large projects, show deposits separately: an advance payment already received, and the balance now due. That transparency is what stops the "I thought I already paid" argument before it starts. Payment terms that get you paid faster

The due date is a term, not a suggestion — so set it deliberately. "Net 14" or "Net 30" are the standard choices; "due on receipt" is fair for small jobs and short projects. Shorter terms get you paid faster, but they only work if the client agreed to them before the work started, so put the terms in your quote or contract as well as on the invoice. State how to pay in plain language: bank transfer details, the payment email for PayPal or a similar service, or a payment link. List every method you accept, and put the one you prefer first. Add a late fee policy in one line — for example, "A 2% monthly late fee applies to overdue balances" — and only add it if you are willing to enforce it. A late fee you never charge is worse than no late fee at all, because it teaches the client the deadline is flexible. Finally, add a short thank-you line and your name at the bottom. It costs one sentence and it makes the next invoice easier to send.

Tax: what to include and what to check

Whether you add tax to an invoice depends on where you live, where your client is based, and whether you are registered for sales tax or VAT. Some freelancers add a tax line, some invoice without tax, and some clients handle tax themselves through reverse-charge rules. There is no single right answer, which is why the template matters: build the tax line into the template once, with the rule you follow, and you will not have to decide it fresh for every invoice. If you are unsure, check your local tax authority's guidance or ask an accountant — a wrong tax line is far more expensive to fix than a quick question is to ask. Common mistakes to avoid

  • No invoice number — without one, you and the client cannot refer to the same invoice.
  • Vague line items — "design work" invites questions; "landing page design, 3 pages" does not.
  • No due date — an invoice without a due date is an invoice the client pays whenever they feel like it.
  • Missing payment details — if the client has to email you to ask how to pay, the payment is already late.
  • Different formats for every client — inconsistency looks unprofessional and makes your own records harder to keep.
  • Sending the invoice as an editable document — always send a PDF, so the client cannot change the amounts by accident.
  • Forgetting to follow up — send a polite reminder the day after the due date, then weekly. Most late payments are forgetfulness, not refusal.

Your freelance invoice template, in short

The total due. Your payment details and terms. A tax line if one applies. Send it as a PDF, the same day the work is delivered, and follow up politely if the due date passes. Frequently asked questions

What should a freelance invoice template include?

Every freelance invoice needs your name and contact details, the client's name and address, a unique invoice number, the invoice date and due date, a clear description of each service, the amount for each line, the total due, and your payment details. Adding your payment terms and a tax note, where applicable, keeps the invoice complete and professional.

Do freelancers need to charge tax on invoices?

It depends on where you live and where your client is based. Some freelancers add sales tax or VAT, others do not charge tax at all, and some clients handle tax through reverse-charge rules. Check your local tax rules or ask an accountant before you add a tax line, and keep a note of the rule you followed on each invoice.
