Invoice Numbering: How to Number Invoices (2026)

Updated 19 November 2026 · 8-minute read

Quick answer: Give every invoice a unique, sequential number from one unbroken series — for example INV-2026-0001, INV-2026-0002, and so on — and never reuse a number or renumber an invoice after it has been sent. Include the year so the numbers sort and file cleanly, keep the format identical on every invoice, and if you skip a number by mistake, leave the gap, note it in your records, and carry on. Unique sequential numbers are required in many jurisdictions, and they are also the simplest way to track what has been paid, what is overdue, and what is missing.

Invoice numbering looks like the least interesting part of freelancing — a small number in the corner of a document — until it goes wrong. A client pays the wrong invoice because two of them share a number. An accountant asks why INV-0041 does not exist. A tax return needs a complete sequence and yours has a hole in it. None of these are dramatic problems, but all of them cost time, and all of them are avoidable with a system that takes five minutes to set up and almost no effort to keep. This guide explains why invoice numbers matter, the two rules every system must follow, the formats that work best, what to do about gaps and duplicates, how to handle numbering across years and clients, and how the free invoice generator on this site handles numbering for you. One note first: this post is general information, not legal or tax advice. The rules on invoice numbering vary by jurisdiction, so for a specific question about what applies where you are based, check with a qualified adviser.

Why invoice numbers matter

An invoice number is the one thing that makes an invoice findable. A client who wants to pay you looks for the number. An accountant who wants to check your books asks for the numbers. A dispute about whether a payment was made is settled by the number. Without numbers, invoices are just dates and amounts — and two invoices for the same amount, sent in the same month, are impossible to tell apart. Numbers turn a pile of documents into a ledger you can search, sort and check.

There is a second reason, and it is the one that matters most: in many jurisdictions, invoices must be numbered uniquely and sequentially, with no gaps, because a clean sequence is how a tax authority checks that no invoice has been hidden or deleted. A missing number looks like a missing invoice, and a missing invoice looks like unreported income — even when the real explanation is that you skipped a number by accident. Keeping a clean sequence is therefore not just good housekeeping; it is the simplest way to show that your records are complete. Even where sequential numbering is not a strict legal requirement, a single unbroken series is the easiest system to keep and the easiest to explain, so treat it as the default.

The two rules every numbering system must follow

Every good numbering system follows two rules, and everything else is a matter of taste. Rule one: every number is unique. No two invoices ever share a number, not even invoices for different clients, different years, or different kinds of work. A number that appears twice is worse than no number at all, because it makes two different debts look like one. Rule two: numbers run in sequence, with no gaps. Each new invoice takes the next number after the last one, and the sequence never jumps, restarts, or goes backwards. A gap is not a crime, but it is a question — and every question from an accountant or a tax authority is a conversation you would rather not have.

Those two rules decide almost every choice in this guide. A format that makes duplicates easy is a bad format. A system that restarts the sequence for each client is a system that creates gaps. A habit of deleting a cancelled invoice and reusing its number breaks both rules at once. Keep the two rules in front of you whenever you are tempted to improvise, and most numbering problems disappear.

Common invoice number formats

There is no single correct format. What matters is that the format is short, consistent, and easy to keep for years. These are the formats freelancers use most often, in order from simplest to most detailed:

  • Plain sequence — 0001, 0002, 0003. The simplest possible system: start at 1 and count up. It works well if you send few invoices, but it tells you nothing about when an invoice was sent, and after a few years the numbers get long and hard to file by year.
  • Prefix + sequence — INV-0001, INV-0002. The most common format for freelancers. The prefix INV makes it obvious the document is an invoice, and the zero-padded sequence — 0001 rather than 1 — keeps the numbers in the right order when they are sorted as text. A plain "INV-1" sorts after "INV-10" in most software; "INV-0001" does not.
  • Prefix + year + sequence — INV-2026-0001, INV-2026-0002. The format this guide recommends. The year makes the number easy to sort, file and search, it tells you at a glance when an invoice was sent, and it keeps each year's numbers short. It is the format the examples in this guide use.
  • Year + month + sequence — 2026-11-001. Useful if you send many invoices and want to know the month at a glance. It is longer, and it is only worth it if you genuinely file and search by month.
  • Client code + sequence — ACME-001. Tempting, but usually a mistake: it creates a separate series for every client, which makes gaps and duplicates more likely, and it makes it harder to show one complete sequence. See the section on clients below.

Whichever format you choose, write it down once and never change it mid-year. Changing the format in the middle of a year creates two series in one year, which is exactly the kind of mess the two rules are meant to prevent. If you want to change formats, do it on 1 January, and note the change in your records.

Starting your sequence: what number to begin with

Begin at 1 — or, if you want your first invoice to look less like a first invoice, begin at a round number such as 100 or 1000. There is no rule against starting at INV-2026-0100, and many freelancers do it because a three-digit starting number looks more established to a new client. What matters is not where you start but that you start once and count up from there without ever going back. Do not start at 1, send five invoices, then decide you would rather have started at 100 and renumber the first five. Those five invoices are already out in the world, with their numbers on them, and renumbering them creates five duplicates and five gaps at the same time.

If you are switching from an old system — paper invoices, a spreadsheet, another tool — carry the sequence over. Find the highest number you have used so far, and start the new system at the next number. Do not restart at 1 in the new tool, or you will have two different invoices with the same number in the same year. If your old system had no numbers at all, start at 1 now and note in your records that numbering began on that date.

Gaps and duplicates: what to do when numbering goes wrong

Mistakes happen. The question is what you do about them, and the answer is almost always the same: do nothing to the numbers, and write down what happened.

A skipped number. You meant to send INV-2026-0042 and sent INV-2026-0043 instead, or you created a draft, deleted it, and the number went with it. Leave the gap. Do not reuse the skipped number for the next invoice, and do not renumber the invoices that came after it. Add a one-line note to your records — "INV-2026-0042 skipped: draft deleted before sending, 19 November 2026" — and carry on from the next number. A documented gap is easy to explain to an accountant or a tax authority. A renumbered invoice is not.

A duplicate number. Two invoices went out with the same number, usually because two drafts were created at the same time or the same invoice was sent twice. Do not delete one of them and pretend it never existed. Instead, correct the record: if one of the two was sent in error, cancel it in writing — tell the client the invoice is void, keep a copy of the voided invoice, and note the reason. The other invoice keeps the number. If both invoices are real and both need to be paid, give one of them a new number — the next number in the sequence — and tell the client in writing that the number has changed and why. What you must never do is leave two live invoices with the same number: sooner or later one of them will be paid and the other will look paid too.

A cancelled or voided invoice. A cancelled invoice keeps its number forever. Do not reuse the number, and do not delete the invoice from your records. Mark it clearly as void, keep the file, and note the reason and the date. A voided invoice with a number and a note is a complete record. A missing number is a hole.

A wrong number on a sent invoice. If you discover a typo in a number after the invoice has been sent, do not quietly send a corrected copy with a different number and no explanation. Tell the client in writing, cancel the wrong-numbered invoice, and issue a new one with the next number in the sequence, referencing the cancelled one. It takes one email, and it keeps the sequence clean and the client informed.

Numbering across years and clients

Across years: there are two sensible approaches, and you should pick one and stick to it. The first is to keep counting — if 2026 ends at INV-2026-0142, then 2027 starts at INV-2027-0143. The sequence never breaks, which is the simplest system of all. The second is to restart the sequence each year — 2026 ends at INV-2026-0142, and 2027 starts at INV-2027-0001. This is also fine, and it is the more common choice, because the year in the number makes it obvious that the sequence restarted. What is not fine is restarting the sequence without the year in the number, or restarting it at a random point in the year: either one creates duplicates. If you use the INV-2026-0001 format, a yearly restart is safe, because the year is part of the number. Pick one approach, write it down, and do not change it.

Across clients: use one sequence for all clients. It is tempting to give each client their own series — ACME-001, BETA-001 — because it looks tidy, but it creates more problems than it solves. Separate series make gaps and duplicates more likely, because there are more sequences to keep track of, and they make it harder to prove your invoices form one complete record. A client does not care that their invoice is number 42 in your whole sequence rather than number 3 in their series; they care that the number is unique and that it matches the invoice they received. If you want to tell clients apart at a glance, use the client name and the date on the invoice, not a separate number series. One sequence, every client, every year.

Across kinds of work: the same rule applies. Do not run one series for design work and another for consulting, or one for hourly work and another for fixed fees. One sequence for all invoices, whatever the work is. If you need to tell kinds of work apart, use the invoice description, not the number.

Record-keeping: the list behind the numbers

A numbering system is only as good as the record behind it. Keep a simple list — a spreadsheet is enough — with one row per invoice: the number, the date, the client, the amount, the due date, and the status (sent, paid, overdue, void). Update it every time you send an invoice, not at the end of the month. The list is what lets you check, at a glance, that the numbers run in sequence with no gaps and no duplicates, and it is what your accountant will ask for first.

Keep the invoices themselves, too: a copy of every invoice you send, in order, in one folder, named by number. Keep voided invoices in the same folder, marked as void, not deleted. Keep the list and the folder together for as long as your jurisdiction requires you to keep business records — often several years — and back both up. A numbering system with no records behind it is a system nobody can check, and a system nobody can check is a system that will fail the first time someone asks about it.

How to number invoices, in short

Give every invoice a unique, sequential number from one unbroken series — INV-2026-0001 is a good format — and never reuse a number or renumber an invoice after it has been sent. Frequently asked questions

Do invoice numbers have to be sequential?

In many jurisdictions, yes — invoices must carry unique, sequential numbers with no gaps, because a clean sequence is how tax authorities check that no invoice has been hidden or deleted. Even where it is not a strict legal rule, a single unbroken sequence is the simplest system to keep and the easiest to defend, so treat sequential numbering as the default unless you have a clear reason to do otherwise.

What is the best format for an invoice number?

The best format is a short, consistent one you can keep for years: a prefix such as INV, then a year, then a zero-padded sequence, for example INV-2026-0042. The year makes the number easy to sort and file, the zero padding keeps the numbers in order when sorted as text, and the prefix tells anyone reading it that the document is an invoice. Pick one format and never change it mid-year.

What should I do if I skip an invoice number by mistake?

Do not reuse the skipped number and do not renumber the invoices that came after it. Keep the sequence as it is, note the gap in your records with a one-line explanation, and carry on from the next number. A documented gap is easy to explain to an accountant or tax authority; a renumbered invoice, or a number used twice, is much harder to explain.

Can I use a different invoice number series for each client?

You can, but for most freelancers it is better not to. Separate series per client make gaps and duplicates more likely, and they make it harder to prove your invoices form one complete sequence. A single sequence for all clients is simpler and safer; if you want to tell clients apart, use the client name and the invoice date on the document, not a separate number series.
