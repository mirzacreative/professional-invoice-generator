Hourly vs Project Pricing: How to Invoice Services

Updated 25 November 2026 · 12-minute read

Quick answer: Hourly pricing charges for the time you spend, at an agreed rate, and the invoice shows the hours, the rate, and the total. Project pricing — also called fixed pricing — charges one agreed price for a defined set of deliverables, and the invoice shows that price, in the instalments agreed in advance. Hourly pricing protects you when the scope is uncertain, because you are paid for the time the work actually takes. Project pricing protects the client when the scope is clear, because the client knows the cost before the work starts, and it rewards you for working efficiently. Neither model is right for every job, and most experienced freelancers use both, choosing the model job by job. This guide explains how each model works, how each is quoted, tracked, and invoiced, how to work the arithmetic with illustrative examples, how to handle scope changes under each model, and how to choose. It is general information, not legal or tax advice, and the rules that apply to invoicing, tax, and record-keeping vary by jurisdiction, so check the rules where you are based and ask a qualified adviser for a specific question.

The pricing question arrives before the invoicing question, and it decides what the invoice will look like. A client asks, "How much will this cost?" and the freelancer must answer in a way that is honest, checkable, and fair to both sides. Answering with an hourly rate alone leaves the client unable to budget, because the total depends on hours the client cannot predict. Answering with a fixed price alone, for work whose scope is still unknown, leaves the freelancer carrying all the risk of a job that grows, and the client exposed to a price that was guessed before the work was understood. The habit that avoids both traps is a short, written decision, made before the work starts: what is the deliverable, what is included and excluded, which pricing model applies, what the rate or price is, how the work will be tracked or measured, and when and how payment will be asked for. That decision, written in a quote or a written confirmation, becomes the invoice. The invoice should never contain a surprise, because the invoice should never contain anything the quote did not already say. The rest of this guide turns that habit into a working method for each model, with the invoice guides on this site linked where they cover a step in depth: how to write an invoice, payment terms, invoice numbering, sending an invoice by email, and chasing a late payment.

What Hourly Pricing Means

Hourly pricing is the simplest model to describe: you agree a rate for an hour of your time, you record the time you spend, and you invoice for the time recorded. The rate is stated in advance, in writing, with the currency, and the invoice shows the calculation so the client can check it: hours multiplied by rate, plus any tax, equals the total. Hourly pricing is transparent in one direction and uncertain in another. It is transparent because the client can see exactly what was done and for how long, provided the time record is kept properly. It is uncertain because the final total is not known until the work is done, and a job that takes longer than expected costs more than the client hoped, even if every hour was genuinely needed. That uncertainty is the model's main weakness, and it is managed by the controls described later in this guide: a cap, a checkpoint, regular updates, and a clear definition of billable time.

Hourly pricing suits work where the end point cannot be fixed in advance. Discovery work, research, troubleshooting, support, maintenance, open-ended design exploration, and work where the client expects to direct the work as it goes are all natural hourly jobs, because the scope is the time itself. A client who says, "Spend up to ten hours investigating this problem and tell me what you find," is asking for hourly work, and a fixed price for that request would be a guess. Hourly pricing also suits a new client relationship, where trust has not yet been built, because the client pays only for time shown to have been spent, and the freelancer is not asked to commit to a price for a client whose working style is unknown. It suits small, irregular jobs, where the cost of writing a detailed fixed-price quote would be larger than the job itself, and it suits work that is genuinely measured in time, such as attending meetings, reviewing documents, or providing cover for an absent member of staff. The test is whether the work is better described by the time it takes than by a deliverable that can be listed. If the deliverable can be listed, counted, and checked, a project price may be the better choice, as the next section explains.

Hourly pricing has a cost for the freelancer that is easy to overlook. It rewards slowness and penalises speed, because a faster freelancer, doing the same job in fewer hours, is paid less than a slower one. It caps income at the number of hours that can be worked, which is a hard limit, and it ties income to presence, so a week without billable hours is a week without income, however much experience the freelancer has. It also creates an administrative burden: every hour must be recorded, categorised, and justified, and a freelancer who does not keep that record will find the invoice questioned and the payment delayed. Those costs do not make hourly pricing wrong. They make it a model to use deliberately, for the jobs it suits, with the controls that keep it fair, rather than a default for every job. A freelancer who charges hourly for a job that could have been priced as a project is not being generous. The freelancer is giving away the value of experience, and inviting the client to judge the work by the clock rather than by the result.

What Project Pricing Means

Project pricing, also called fixed pricing or flat-fee pricing, charges one agreed price for a defined set of deliverables. The price is agreed before the work starts, in a quote or a written confirmation that lists the deliverables, the price, the validity period, the exclusions, and the payment instalments. The invoice then follows the agreement: the fixed price, in the instalments agreed, with any agreed change shown separately and any deposit credited. The client can budget with certainty, because the price is known before the work starts, and the freelancer can plan with certainty, because the income for the job is known, provided the scope does not change. The risk moves from the client to the freelancer in one respect: if the job takes longer than expected, the freelancer absorbs the extra time, because the price does not rise with the hours. That risk is the model's main weakness, and it is managed by defining the scope tightly, by pricing the risk into the figure, and by handling changes through a written change process, as described later.

Project pricing suits work whose deliverables can be listed, counted, and checked. A logo with a stated number of concepts and revision rounds, a website with a stated number of pages and features, a report with a stated length and structure, a set of social media templates with a stated count, and a translation with a stated word count are all natural project-priced jobs, because the client and the freelancer can agree, before the work starts, what "done" means. Project pricing also suits repeat work, where the same deliverable is produced for different clients, because the freelancer knows how long the work takes and can price it from experience rather than from a guess. It suits clients who need approval from someone else, because a fixed figure can be approved, while an open-ended hourly estimate often cannot. And it suits work where the value is in the result, not in the time: a design that solves a problem in two hours, after years of learning how to solve it in two hours, is worth more than two hours at an hourly rate, and a project price is the way to charge for the result rather than for the clock.

Project pricing has its own cost for the client, which should be acknowledged honestly. A fixed price includes a margin for the freelancer's risk, so a job that turns out to be simple may cost more on a project price than it would have cost by the hour, and the client cannot know that in advance. A fixed price also requires the client to define the scope before the work starts, which takes effort and thought, and a client who cannot define the scope may find the quote process frustrating. And a fixed price can create a dispute about scope, because the line between "included" and "extra" is the line the whole price depends on. Those costs do not make project pricing wrong either. They make it a model to use for defined work, with a scope written carefully enough that both sides can point to the same document, and with a change process that handles the inevitable moment when the client asks for something more.

Hourly vs Project Pricing at a Glance

The comparison below summarises the two models, so that the choice can be made job by job, in a few minutes, rather than by habit. Read it as a guide to the trade-off, not as a rule that decides every job, because most real jobs sit somewhere between the two models, and the hybrid and milestone models in the next section cover that middle ground.

Hourly: the unit is time, usually an hour, sometimes a half-hour or a day. The price is a rate, stated in advance. The total is unknown until the work is done, and is calculated from the time record. The client carries the risk of a job taking longer than expected, and the freelancer carries the risk of not being paid for unrecorded time. The control is the time record, the cap, and the checkpoint. The invoice shows dates, tasks, hours, rate, and total. It suits open, exploratory, support, and irregular work. Project: the unit is a deliverable, or a set of deliverables. The price is a fixed figure, stated in advance. The total is known before the work starts, and is paid in instalments. The freelancer carries the risk of a job taking longer than expected, and the client carries the risk of paying for a scope that turns out to be simpler than expected. The control is the scope definition and the change process. The invoice shows the fixed price, the instalments, any agreed change, and any deposit credit. It suits defined, repeatable, and value-based work. The shared requirements are the same for both models: a written agreement before the work starts, a clear statement of what is included and excluded, agreed payment terms, and an invoice that matches the agreement. A model chosen without those shared requirements will cause a dispute, whichever model it is.

Other Models: Day Rates, Retainers, Milestones, and Hybrids

Most freelance work does not fit neatly into hourly or project pricing alone, and three further models cover the common middle ground. A day rate is hourly pricing with a larger unit: a price for a day's work, usually with a stated number of hours and a definition of what a day includes. Day rates suit work that is bought in whole days, such as on-site support, training, or a day of filming, where an hourly rate would be fiddly and a project price would be artificial. A retainer is a fixed monthly fee for a stated amount of work, or for availability, usually with a stated number of hours or deliverables per month, a rollover rule for unused time, and a notice period. Retainers suit ongoing work, such as monthly design, monthly content, or ongoing support, and they give both sides a predictable rhythm. Our guide to recurring invoices covers the invoicing rhythm for a retainer or a fixed monthly fee, including how to handle a month where the work differs from the retainer.

Milestone pricing is project pricing broken into stages: the total is fixed, but it is paid in instalments tied to agreed milestones, such as approval of a design, delivery of a draft, or launch of a site. Milestone pricing suits larger projects, because it spreads the client's risk and the freelancer's cash flow across the job, and it gives both sides a natural point to review the scope before the next stage. Hybrid pricing combines the models: an hourly rate for the discovery phase, a project price for the defined deliverable that follows, or a project price for the core deliverable with an hourly rate for extras and changes. Hybrids are the most honest model for many jobs, because the early part of a job is often exploratory and the later part is defined, and a single model for both parts fits neither. Whatever hybrid is chosen, the boundary must be written down: which part is hourly, which part is project-priced, where the boundary falls, and what happens when work crosses it. A hybrid without a written boundary is two models arguing with each other, and the invoice will show it.

Worked Examples: The Arithmetic, Labelled Illustrative

Every figure in this section is illustrative and invented for the example only. The names, rates, hours, and prices are made up, to show the arithmetic. Replace every figure with your own, keep the structure, and check the calculation before you send a quote or an invoice.

Hourly example. A freelance editor agrees an hourly rate of 40.00 in the local currency, with time recorded in half-hour units, a weekly invoice, and a checkpoint at 10 hours, at which the editor will stop and report before continuing. In the first week, the editor records 3.5 hours on Monday, 4 hours on Tuesday, and 2.5 hours on Thursday, a total of 10 hours, and reaches the checkpoint. The invoice for the week shows the three dated entries, the hours, the rate, and the line total: 10 hours at 40.00 equals 400.00, plus tax where it applies, shown separately. The client can check the total from the record, the editor has reached the checkpoint without exceeding it, and the next phase is agreed before more time is spent. If the same work had been priced as a project, the editor would have had to guess the hours before reading the material, and a guess that was too low would have left the editor unpaid for real work, while a guess that was too high would have left the client overpaying for a short job. The hourly model, with a checkpoint, fits the uncertainty.

Project example. A freelance designer agrees a fixed price of 900.00 for a defined deliverable: a logo with three concepts, two revision rounds, and final files in two formats, with a deposit of 50% before work starts and the balance on delivery. The quote lists the deliverables, the exclusions, including stock photography licences at cost, and the payment instalments. The designer invoices 450.00 as a deposit before the work starts, on an invoice or a proforma invoice as the circumstances require, and invoices the balance of 450.00 on delivery, crediting the deposit on the face of the final invoice, so that the balance due is shown plainly. If the client asks for a third revision round, the designer agrees the extra round and its price in writing before doing the work, and shows it as a separate line on the final invoice, so that the fixed price and the agreed extra are both visible. The total the client pays is the fixed price plus the agreed extra, and the invoice explains itself without a query.

Hybrid example. A freelance developer agrees an hourly rate of 50.00 for a discovery phase of up to 8 hours, to investigate a website problem and report the fix, followed by a project price for the fix itself, quoted after the discovery report. The discovery invoice shows the hours recorded, up to the 8-hour cap, and the discovery report states the fix, the project price, and the exclusions. The client approves the project price in writing, the fix is delivered, and the project invoice shows the fixed price, with the discovery hours already billed separately and not charged again. The hybrid works because each phase uses the model that fits it: the unknown part is hourly, the defined part is project-priced, and the boundary — the discovery report — is a document both sides can point to. The arithmetic in each phase is simple, and the simplicity is the point. A pricing model that needs a spreadsheet to explain is a pricing model the client will query, and a query delays payment.

Setting Your Rate: The Calculation Behind the Figure

A rate or a project price should be built from a calculation, not copied from another freelancer or guessed from a client's budget. The calculation starts with the income the freelancer needs, including the costs of running the business, the time that is not billable, and the provision for tax, and it ends with a figure that can be stated with confidence. For an hourly rate, the simplest form of the calculation is to take the annual income needed, add the annual business costs, and divide by the number of billable hours that can realistically be worked in the year, allowing for holidays, illness, administration, marketing, and learning, which are real work but are not billed to a client. The result is a starting point, not a final answer, and it should be checked against the market for the skill and the location, and against the value of the work, before it is quoted. A rate that is too low will not cover the costs, however busy the freelancer is, and a rate that is too high for the market will lose work that a lower rate would have won. The calculation gives the floor. The market and the value give the ceiling. The quoted rate sits between them, and it should be reviewed regularly, not set once and forgotten.

For a project price, the calculation starts from the same place, but it is expressed differently. Estimate the hours the defined work is likely to take, based on similar past work, multiply by the hourly rate from the calculation above, add any direct costs, such as stock licences, travel, or subcontracted work, and add a margin for the risk that the job takes longer than estimated or that the scope is less clear than it looks. The result is the project price, and it should be checked against the value to the client and against the market for the same deliverable. If the calculated price is far above the market, the scope may need to be reduced, or the deliverable redefined, so that the price and the scope match. If the calculated price is far below the market, the freelancer may be underpricing experience, and the price should be raised, with the scope and the quality stated plainly so the client can see what the price buys. In both cases, the quote should show the deliverables and the price, not the calculation. A client does not need to see the freelancer's cost breakdown, and a quote that shows the hours, the rate, and the margin invites a negotiation about the freelancer's costs, which is not the client's business. The client needs to see what will be delivered, for what price, by when, and on what terms. The calculation is for the freelancer. The quote is for the client.

Review the rate and the project prices regularly, at least once a year, and after any significant change in costs, skills, or demand. A rate that was right two years ago may be wrong now, because costs have risen, skills have deepened, or the market has moved, and a freelancer who never reviews the rate will gradually be paid less, in real terms, for better work. When the rate changes, tell existing clients in advance, in writing, and apply the new rate to new work, not to work already quoted at the old rate. A client who receives an invoice at a rate higher than the rate quoted, without warning, will query it, and the query is justified. A client who is told, politely and in advance, that the rate will change from a stated date, and who agrees the new rate for the next job, will accept it. The same applies to project prices: a repeat job at the same price, year after year, while costs rise, is a price cut in disguise, and it should be reviewed, with the client, before the next repeat, not after it has been invoiced.

Choosing the Model: Six Questions for Each Job

Choose the model by answering six questions about the job, in writing, before the quote is sent. The answers will usually point clearly to one model, and when they do not, a hybrid will usually be the honest answer.

1. Can the deliverable be listed? If the deliverable can be listed, counted, and checked — a number of pages, a number of templates, a number of concepts — a project price is possible. If the deliverable cannot be listed, because the job is to investigate, to advise, or to support, hourly pricing is the honest model. 2. How certain is the scope? If the scope is certain, and unlikely to change, a project price is fair. If the scope is uncertain, or likely to change as the work progresses, hourly pricing, or a hybrid with an hourly discovery phase, is fairer to both sides. 3. Who carries the risk? If the client wants certainty of cost, and is willing to pay a price that includes a risk margin, a project price gives it. If the freelancer cannot carry the risk of an unknown job, hourly pricing shares the risk more fairly. 4. How will the work be judged? If the work will be judged by the result — a design, a report, a working site — a project price judges it by the same standard. If the work will be judged by the time spent — cover, support, attendance — an hourly rate judges it by the same standard. 5. What does the client need for approval? If the client needs a fixed figure for approval, a project price, or a capped hourly estimate with a checkpoint, is needed. If the client can approve time as it is spent, hourly pricing works. 6. What is the history? If the work is repeat work, with a known duration and a known scope, a project price, or a retainer, is efficient. If the work is new, with an unknown client or an unknown problem, hourly pricing, with a checkpoint, is safer. Write the answers down, in a few lines, for each job. The written answers are the reasoning behind the quote, and they will help you to explain the choice to the client, and to review it if the job changes.

Two further factors deserve a mention. The first is the client's working style. A client who gives clear briefs, answers questions promptly, and approves work on time is a good candidate for project pricing, because the scope will stay stable. A client who briefs vaguely, changes direction often, and answers slowly is a poor candidate for a fixed price, because the scope will change, and the freelancer will absorb the cost of the changes unless the change process is followed rigorously. In that case, hourly pricing, or a project price with a strict change process, is the safer choice, and the choice should be made honestly, before the work starts, not after the third change. The second factor is the freelancer's own experience. A freelancer who has done the same job many times can price it as a project with confidence, because the duration is known. A freelancer who is doing a job for the first time cannot know the duration, and should not pretend otherwise. An hourly rate, with a cap and a checkpoint, is the honest model for a first-time job, and the experience gained will make the next similar job project-priced with confidence.

Quoting and Invoicing Hourly Work, Step by Step

Quote hourly work in writing, with the rate, the rounding rule, the definition of billable time, the invoicing frequency, the cap or checkpoint, and the payment terms. State the currency, state whether tax is included or added, and state what is not billable, such as the freelancer's own learning, or travel time, if that is the agreement. State how time will be reported: a weekly summary, a time record attached to the invoice, or access to a shared time log. State what happens at the cap: work stops, the client is told, and further work is agreed before it is done. A quote that says only "40.00 per hour" is not a quote. It is a rate, and a rate without controls is an open-ended commitment that neither side can budget for. Our quote-versus-invoice guide explains the quote document in full, and the hourly quote should follow the same structure, with the hourly controls added.

Track the time as you work, not at the end of the week from memory. Record each entry on the day it happens, with the date, the start and end times or the duration, the task, and the client or job, using a timer, a time-tracking tool, or a simple notebook, whichever you will actually use. Round the time as agreed, and round consistently: if the agreement is to round to the nearest half-hour, round every entry the same way, and do not round up every entry, which inflates the total and will be noticed. Review the time record weekly, check it against the cap, and tell the client before the cap is reached, not after it. A client who is told, "We are at 8 of the 10 agreed hours, and the work will need a further 5 hours," can make a decision. A client who is told, after the event, "The job took 15 hours, not 10," cannot, and will query the invoice. The update is part of the service, not an extra.

Invoice hourly work from the time record, on the frequency agreed, showing the dated entries, the hours, the rate, the line totals, the subtotal, any tax, and the total due, with the payment terms and the due date, as explained in our how-to-write-an-invoice guide and our payment terms guide. Attach the time record, or summarise it on the invoice, so the client can check the total without asking. Use the next invoice number, in one sequence, as explained in our invoice numbering guide, and send the invoice by the method the client prefers, as explained in our guide to sending an invoice by email. File the time record, the quote, and the invoice together, per client, per job, so that any query can be answered from the file. If the client queries an entry, answer from the record, politely and specifically, and correct any genuine error promptly, with a credit note if the invoice has already been sent. A freelancer who corrects an error quickly, without argument, earns more trust than a freelancer who defends a record that is wrong.

Quoting and Invoicing Project Work, Step by Step

Quote project work in writing, with the deliverables listed, the price stated, the instalments stated, the exclusions named, the validity period given, and the change process stated. List each deliverable in a way that can be checked: a count, a format, a length, or a feature, so that "done" is a fact, not an opinion. State the number of revision rounds included, and the price of further rounds, so that a revision request does not become a dispute. State the assumptions: what the client will supply, by when, and what will happen if the supply is late. State the payment instalments, for example a deposit before work starts, a payment at a milestone, and the balance on delivery, with the dates or triggers for each. State the payment terms that will appear on the invoice, so the client agrees them before the work starts. And state the validity period, so the price does not stand indefinitely. A project quote that omits any of those items will cause a question later, and the question will arrive when the invoice is sent, which is the worst time for it.

Track project work against the deliverables, not against the clock, but keep a private record of the time spent, for your own pricing of the next similar job. The private time record is not billed to the client, and it should not be shown on the invoice, but it is the evidence that will tell you whether the next project price for the same deliverable should be higher, lower, or the same. Review the scope at each milestone, and raise any change before it is done, as the next section explains. Deliver the work as described, in the format described, by the date described, or tell the client in advance if the date will slip, with a revised date. A project price is a promise about the deliverable, the price, and the date, and a freelancer who keeps all three, or who warns early when one will slip, will be trusted with the next project. A freelancer who delivers late, without warning, and invoices the full price, will not.

Invoice project work in the instalments agreed, showing the fixed price, the instalment, any agreed change as a separate line, any deposit already received credited on the face of the invoice, and the balance due, with the payment terms and the due date. The first invoice, for a deposit, should be sent before the work starts, if that is the agreement, and should refer to the quote reference, so the client can match it to the approval. The final invoice should show the whole picture: the fixed price, less the deposit received, plus any agreed extras, equals the balance due. A final invoice that shows only the balance, without the fixed price and the deposit credit, will be queried, because the client cannot see how the balance was reached. If payment is late, follow the process in our late payment guide, referring to the invoice number, the amount, and the due date, and attaching the invoice again.

Scope Changes: The Moment That Decides the Model

Scope changes are the moment when a pricing model is tested, and they are the most common cause of freelance payment disputes. A client asks for one more revision, one more page, one more feature, or a faster deadline, and the freelancer, wanting to be helpful, does the extra work without agreeing a price. The invoice then contains a figure the client never agreed to, the client queries it, and the relationship suffers, however good the work was. The rule for both models is the same, and it is simple: agree the change, and the price, in writing, before you do the extra work. For hourly work, the change usually means more hours, and the agreement is about the cap: the cap is raised, or a new cap is set, and the client is told the new total the hours will reach. For project work, the change usually means a revised or supplementary quote, stating what has changed, what the new price is, and that the original quote no longer applies to the changed part of the job, with the client's written acceptance before the work proceeds.

Small changes deserve the same discipline as large ones, in proportion. A small change can be agreed in a short email exchange, provided the exchange is in writing, states the change and the price, and receives a written reply. A large change deserves a revised quote, with the same care as the original. The test is whether the change could be disputed later: if it could, write it down. If the client asks for a change and the freelancer believes it is already included in the scope, say so, politely, and point to the quote line that includes it. If the client disagrees, the quote is the evidence, and the discussion will be about the wording of the quote, which is a discussion that can be resolved. If there is no written scope, the discussion will be about memory, which cannot be resolved. Keep a change log for each job, with the date, the change, the price agreed, and the client's acceptance, filed with the quote and the invoice. The change log is the audit trail for the job, and it is what you will want if the final invoice is ever questioned.

There is one exception to the rule, and it should be used sparingly: a tiny change, of a few minutes, that the freelancer chooses to absorb as goodwill, without charging. Absorbing a tiny change is good client care, provided it is genuinely tiny, and provided the client is told, politely, that it has been absorbed as goodwill, so that the next tiny change is not expected free as well. Absorbing every change as goodwill is not good client care. It is unpaid work, and it trains the client to expect free work, which damages the relationship in the long run. The balance is a matter of judgement, job by job, and the judgement should be made consciously, not by default. A freelancer who never charges for changes will be busy and poor. A freelancer who charges for every minute will be resented. The middle course — agree the price for real changes, absorb the truly trivial ones, and tell the client which is which — is the course that keeps both the income and the relationship.

Communicating the Price to the Client

How the price is communicated matters as much as the price itself. State the price plainly, in the first page of the quote, with the currency, the model, and the total, so the client does not have to search for it. State what the price includes, in the same place, so the client can see the value. State what it excludes, so the client is not surprised later. State the payment terms, the instalments, and the due dates, so the client can plan. And state how to accept: reply to the email, sign the quote, or confirm in writing. A quote that hides the price on the last page, or that states the price without the scope, invites a misunderstanding, and a misunderstanding invites a dispute. The covering message should be short: the quote is attached, the price covers the items listed, the validity is stated, and the client is asked to confirm acceptance. The quote does the explaining. The message does the asking.

When a client queries a price, answer from the quote, not from feeling. Show the deliverables, the rate or price, the time record or the scope, and the change log, and explain the figure in the same terms as the quote. If the query reveals a genuine error, correct it promptly, with an apology and a corrected invoice or credit note, as the case requires. If the query is a request for a lower price, decide on commercial grounds, not on emotion: can the scope be reduced to meet the lower price, can the instalments be changed, or is the price firm? A reduced scope for a reduced price is a legitimate negotiation, and it should be written down as a revised quote. A reduced price for the same scope is a discount, and it should be recorded as a discount, with the reason, so that the next job is not expected at the discounted price. Never reduce a price by working unpaid hours, or by cutting a corner that the client will notice in the deliverable. A price cut that is paid for by the freelancer's unpaid time is not a negotiation. It is a loss, and it will be repeated.

Common Mistakes With Hourly and Project Pricing

Quoting only a rate, with no cap or checkpoint. An open-ended hourly commitment cannot be budgeted, and the invoice will surprise the client. Always state a cap, a checkpoint, or an estimate with a review point. Quoting a fixed price for undefined work. A fixed price for a scope that is not yet understood is a guess, and the guess will be wrong in one direction or the other. Use hourly pricing, or a hybrid, until the scope is defined. Not recording time on hourly work. Time recorded from memory, at the end of the week, is not a record the client can trust. Record it as you work. Not defining the scope on project work. A vague scope is a dispute waiting to happen. List the deliverables, the revisions, and the exclusions. Doing extra work without agreement. Extra work done without a written agreement is extra work that may not be paid for, and it damages the relationship. Agree the change first. Forgetting the deposit credit. A deposit already received must be credited on the final invoice, in plain figures. Charging the full price again is an error the client will notice. Changing the model retrospectively. A model change applies to future work, by agreement, not to work already done. Do not re-price work already billed. Showing the calculation to the client. The client needs the deliverables and the price, not the freelancer's cost breakdown. Keep the calculation private, and the quote client-facing. Never reviewing the rate. A rate that is never reviewed falls behind costs and experience. Review it at least yearly, and tell existing clients in advance of a change. Mixing the models without a boundary. A hybrid without a written boundary — which part is hourly, which part is project-priced — is two models in conflict. Write the boundary down.

Choosing for Your Next Job: A Short Checklist

Use the checklist below, in order, for the next job you quote. It takes a few minutes, and it will save a dispute later. First, write the deliverable in one sentence, and list what is included and what is excluded. Second, answer the six questions in the choosing section, and note which model they point to. Third, build the figure from the calculation: the rate for hourly work, or the estimated hours plus costs plus a risk margin for project work. Fourth, write the quote, with the model, the rate or price, the cap or instalments, the payment terms, and the validity period, and send it with a short covering message. Fifth, get written acceptance before you start. Sixth, track the work as the model requires: time for hourly work, deliverables and changes for project work. Seventh, update the client at the checkpoint or milestone, before the cap or the scope is exceeded. Eighth, invoice from the quote, in the instalments agreed, crediting any deposit, and file the quote, the acceptance, the record, and the invoice together. Ninth, review the job afterwards: did the model fit, did the price cover the work, and what would you change for the next similar job? That review is how pricing improves, job by job, from a guess to a method. Frequently asked questions

Should I charge hourly or per project for freelance services?

Choose by the job, not by habit. Hourly pricing suits work whose scope is open, exploratory, or likely to change, because the client pays for the time actually used and the freelancer is not locked into a price for unknown work. Project pricing suits work whose deliverables are clear, measurable, and unlikely to change, because the client can budget a fixed figure and the freelancer is rewarded for working efficiently. Many freelancers use both: hourly for discovery, support, and open-ended work, and project pricing for defined deliverables. State the model, the rate or price, and what is included in writing before the work starts, so the invoice matches what was agreed.

How do I invoice hourly work?

Record the time as you work, in dated entries that name the task, then invoice from that record: the dates, the hours, the hourly rate, and the line total, with a subtotal, any tax, and the total due. Agree in advance how time is rounded, what counts as billable, how often you will invoice, and whether there is a cap or a checkpoint at which you will stop and ask the client before continuing. Attach or summarise the time record with the invoice, so the client can check it without asking, and keep the record with the invoice for your own accounts. Our guide on how to write an invoice covers the invoice fields in full.

How do I invoice project-priced work?

Invoice the fixed price agreed in the quote or written confirmation, in the instalments agreed before the work started, for example a deposit before work begins and the balance on completion, or payments at agreed milestones. The invoice should show the deliverables, the fixed price, any agreed change shown separately, any deposit already received credited on the face of the invoice, and the balance due. If the scope changes, agree the change and the price in writing before you do the extra work, and show the change on the invoice, so the client is never surprised by a figure that differs from the quote.

Can I switch from hourly to project pricing mid-job?

Yes, but only by agreement, in writing, and at a clean point in the job, such as the end of a phase or a milestone. Agree what has already been billed on the old model, what remains, what the new model covers, and what the new price or rate is, and confirm it in an email or a revised quote before you continue. Do not change the pricing model retrospectively, and do not apply a new model to work already done under the old one, because that is the fastest way to create a dispute about a bill the client believed was settled.
