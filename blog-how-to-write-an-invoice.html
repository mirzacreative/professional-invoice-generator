How to Write an Invoice: Step-by-Step for Freelancers

Updated 23 November 2026 · 10-minute read

Quick answer: To write an invoice, work through the fields in order: title the document "Invoice", add a unique invoice number and the date, add your details and the client's details, describe the work line by line with quantities and prices, add up the subtotal, show any tax or discount, state the total due, and finish with the payment terms, the due date, and how to pay. Then check it, save a copy, and send it promptly. This guide walks through each field in that order, shows a complete worked example invoice you can copy, explains the totals and the tax line, and covers sending, chasing, and record-keeping. It is general information, not legal or tax advice: invoicing rules vary by jurisdiction, so check the requirements that apply where you are based, and ask a qualified adviser for a specific question.

Writing your first invoice feels harder than it should. The work is done, the client is waiting, and suddenly a blank page is asking for numbers, dates, and wording you have never had to produce before. What number should the invoice have? What exactly should the description say? Where do taxes go, and what happens if you get a field wrong? The anxiety is understandable, because an invoice is not just a request for money. It is a business document that the client's finance team will file, that your accountant will rely on, and that a tax authority may ask to see. A clear, complete invoice gets paid faster and causes fewer questions. A vague or incomplete one comes back with queries, sits in an inbox, or gets paid late for a reason that has nothing to do with the quality of your work. The good news is that writing an invoice is a repeatable process, not a creative one. The fields are the same every time, the order is the same every time, and once you have written one good invoice, every invoice after it is a copy with new details. This guide teaches that process, field by field, with a worked example, so that your first invoice — and your fiftieth — takes minutes, not an evening.

Before You Start: Gather the Details

An invoice is slow to write only when the details are scattered. Before you open a template or a generator, gather five things. First, the agreement: the quote, the email, or the contract that states what you agreed to do, for how much, and on what terms. If the agreement says the price, the payment terms, and the due date, the invoice should match it exactly. An invoice that charges a different amount from the quote, without explanation, is the single most common cause of a payment dispute. Second, the client's details: the exact business or personal name to bill, the billing address, the contact person's name and email, and any reference the client needs on the invoice, such as a purchase order number. Ask the client for these details if you do not have them, and use the name and address they give you, not a guess from a website. Third, the work record: what you did, when you did it, how many hours or units it took, and what was delivered. If you bill by the hour, keep a simple time record as you work, because reconstructing hours at the end of a project is unreliable and invites disagreement. Fourth, your payment details: the bank account, payment link, or payment method the client should use, checked carefully. A wrong digit in a bank account number can delay payment for weeks. Fifth, your last invoice number, so the new invoice takes the next number in the sequence. With those five things in front of you, the writing itself is straightforward. Without them, you will stop halfway through to hunt for a detail, and the invoice will sit unfinished.

One habit saves more time than any template: agree the invoicing details before the work starts. When you accept a job, confirm in writing who to invoice, what address to use, what reference to include, when you will invoice, and when payment is due. That short exchange, in an email, prevents most of the back-and-forth that delays payment later. It also protects the relationship, because the client is never surprised by an invoice, and you are never chasing a detail you should have asked for weeks earlier. If you are writing your first invoice and you have not yet had that exchange, have it now, before you send anything. A one-line email — "Please confirm the billing name, address, and any purchase order number for the invoice" — is a normal business request, and no client will think worse of you for asking it.

Step 1: Title the Document "Invoice"

Put the word Invoice at the top of the document, in a prominent place. This sounds obvious, and it is, but it matters: the title tells the reader, the client's finance team, and anyone filing the document exactly what it is. Do not title it "Bill", "Statement", "Payment Request", or "Work Done", and do not leave the title off altogether. If the document is a different kind of document — a quote, a proforma invoice, or a receipt — title it as that document, not as an invoice. Our guide on the difference between an invoice and a receipt explains why the distinction matters, and our guide to proforma invoices explains when a document should be labelled as a proforma invoice instead. A document that says "Invoice" is a demand for payment for work supplied. A document that says anything else is something else, and mixing the titles up is how a client files an invoice as a quote and never pays it.

If you use a template or a generator, the title will usually be filled in for you. Check it anyway. Templates downloaded from the internet sometimes carry titles such as "Tax Invoice", "Commercial Invoice", or "Sales Invoice", and the right title depends on your jurisdiction and on the type of sale. For most freelance services, "Invoice" is the right title. If your jurisdiction requires the words "Tax Invoice" for invoices that charge tax, use that wording, and check what else the rules require. The title is also the place for your business name and, if you use one, your logo. Put your business name at the top, clearly, so the client knows at a glance who is asking to be paid. A logo is optional, but a consistent, professional layout is not: the invoice should look like it came from a business, even if that business is one person working from home.

Step 2: Add the Invoice Number and the Date

Every invoice needs a unique invoice number and a date. The number identifies the invoice, and it is how you, the client, and your accountant will refer to it. Use one sequence, for all clients, counting up by one each time: 001, 002, 003, or 0001, 0002, 0003, with leading zeros so the numbers sort correctly in a list. Start at 001 or 0001 for your first invoice, and never reuse a number, never skip a number, and never renumber an invoice after it has been sent. Do not restart the sequence for each client, because two clients would then have the same invoice numbers, and your records would be ambiguous. Do not use the date as the number, because two invoices on the same day would collide. And do not use a number from a different sequence — for example, a proforma invoice reference — for a tax invoice. Our guide to invoice numbering covers the rules in detail, including what to do when you need to correct a sent invoice: issue a credit note or a corrected invoice that refers to the original number, and never edit the original after the event.

The invoice date is the date you issue the invoice, not the date the work was done and not the date payment is due. Put the date in full, in a format the client will understand — 23 November 2026, or 23/11/2026 if that is the format your client uses — and be consistent. If the work was done over a period, state the period in the description lines or in a note, so the client can see what the invoice covers. Some invoices also carry a due date, and that date belongs with the payment terms, covered in step 7 below. The invoice date and the due date are different dates, and confusing them is a common beginner mistake: the invoice date starts the clock, and the due date is when the clock runs out. Keep them separate, and state both clearly.

Step 3: Add Your Details and the Client's Details

Next, add the two sets of details that identify the parties to the sale. Your details go first, usually at the top: your business name, your address, your email address, and your phone number. If you are registered for tax, add your tax number or registration number, as your jurisdiction requires. If you trade under a business name that differs from your own name, use the business name, and add your own name if the rules require it. Your details should match the details on your bank account or payment account, so that the payment can be matched to you without confusion. The client's details go next, usually labelled "Bill To" or "Billed To": the client's business or personal name, billing address, and contact person's name and email. Use the exact name and address the client gave you. A client's finance team may reject an invoice addressed to the wrong legal name, or to a trading name when the legal name is required, and that rejection costs days. If the client gave you a purchase order number, a cost code, or a department name, put it with the client's details or in a clearly labelled reference field. That reference is often the key that lets the client's system process the invoice, and leaving it off is a common reason for delay.

Check both sets of details against the agreement before you go on. A misspelled business name, a wrong address, or a missing tax number is a small error that causes a large delay, because the client must ask you to correct it, you must reissue the document, and the payment clock restarts. If you are invoicing a client in another country, check what details their country requires, and whether the invoice needs to state the place of supply or the currency. State the currency clearly on every invoice — write the currency code, such as GBP, USD, or EUR, next to the amounts — because an amount without a currency is ambiguous, and an international client cannot safely pay it. If you agreed the price in one currency, invoice in that currency, and do not convert it without agreeing the conversion with the client first.

Step 4: Describe the Work, Line by Line

The description is the heart of the invoice, and it is where vague wording causes the most trouble. Describe the work line by line, with one line for each item, service, or block of time. Each line needs four things: a clear description, a quantity, a unit price, and a line total. The description should be specific enough that a stranger reading the invoice could tell what was done. "Design work" is too vague. "Logo design: three concepts, two revision rounds, final files in PNG and SVG, delivered 18 November 2026" is specific. "Consulting" is too vague. "Website audit: homepage, services page, and contact page, with written recommendations, 6 hours" is specific. Write the description as if the client's finance team, who did not see the work happen, has to approve it. They need to match the description to the quote, the purchase order, or the agreement, and vague wording makes that match impossible.

For hourly work, state the number of hours, the hourly rate, and the dates the work was done, or attach a time summary. For fixed-price work, state the deliverable and the agreed price, and note any milestones or phases if the invoice covers only part of a larger project. For goods, state the item, the quantity, and the unit price, as you would on any sales invoice. If the invoice covers expenses the client agreed to reimburse — travel, materials, software licences bought for the project — list them as separate lines, with the amount and, if the client asked for it, a copy of the receipt. Do not bury expenses in a single "miscellaneous" line. A finance team cannot approve a miscellaneous line without asking what it contains, and the question will delay payment. If the work changed after the quote — the scope grew, an extra revision was added, or a deadline moved — explain the change in the description or in a short note on the invoice, so the client is not surprised by a figure that differs from the quote. Step 5: Add Up the Subtotal, Tax, and Total

Totals are arithmetic, and arithmetic errors are embarrassing and avoidable. Work in this order: subtotal, then discount if you agreed one, then tax if it applies, then total due. The subtotal is the sum of the line totals, before tax. If you agreed a discount, show it as a separate line, with the amount or the percentage, and subtract it to give the discounted subtotal. Then calculate tax on the correct base, according to the rules that apply where you are based and to the type of sale. Tax rules vary widely by jurisdiction, by whether you are registered for tax, and by whether the client is in the same country or abroad, and this guide cannot state the rule for every reader. Check the rules that apply to you, and if you are unsure, ask a qualified adviser before you issue invoices that charge tax. What matters for the layout is that the tax is shown clearly: the tax rate, the amount of tax, and the basis on which it was calculated, so the client and your accountant can both check it.

Show each figure on its own line, labelled clearly: Subtotal, Discount, Tax, Total Due. Use the same currency throughout, and write the currency code next to the total. Round the figures sensibly — two decimal places for most currencies — and make sure the total is the sum of the figures above it. If you use a generator, it will do the arithmetic for you, but check the result anyway, especially the tax line, because a generator can only calculate what you tell it to calculate. If an advance or deposit was already paid, show it as a separate line after the total: Total, Less advance already received, Balance due. The client must never be charged the full amount again after paying a deposit, and the credit for the advance must be on the face of the invoice, in plain figures. If no advance was paid, there is no need for that line. Finally, state the total due in words as well as figures if your jurisdiction or your client requires it, and keep the figures consistent everywhere they appear — in the lines, in the totals, and in any covering email. A total that differs between the invoice and the email will be queried, and the query will delay payment.

The example below is a complete, worked invoice for a fictional freelance designer. Every figure is illustrative, invented for this example only, and the names, dates, and amounts are made up. Use it as a model for your own invoice: replace every detail with your own, keep the structure, and check each field before you send it. Invoice — INV-014

Line 2: Social media template pack — 12 templates for Instagram and Facebook — Quantity: 1 — Unit price: 180.00 GBP — Line total: 180.00 GBP.
Line 3: Stock photography licence for bakery images, as agreed — Quantity: 1 — Unit price: 35.00 GBP — Line total: 35.00 GBP.
Subtotal: 635.00 GBP. Tax at 20% on the subtotal, for illustration: 127.00 GBP. Total due: 762.00 GBP.
Payment terms: Payment due within 14 days of the invoice date, by 7 December 2026. Bank transfer to the account details below. Please quote invoice number INV-014 with your payment.

Read the example as a checklist, not as a form to copy word for word. The invoice number is unique and sequential. The date is the issue date. The business and client details are complete, with a purchase order reference. The description names the project, the deliverables, and the quote date. Each line has a quantity, a unit price, and a line total. The subtotal, the tax, and the total are shown separately, and the total is the sum of the figures above it. The payment terms state the due date, the method, and the reference to quote. Nothing is vague, nothing is missing, and a finance team reading it can match it to the quote and approve it without asking a question. That is the standard to aim for. If your invoice is for hourly work, the only change is in the description lines: replace the fixed-price lines with hours, the hourly rate, and the dates worked, and keep everything else the same. Step 7: Payment Terms, Due Date, and How to Pay

An invoice without payment terms is an invitation to pay late. State the terms clearly, on the face of the invoice, in plain language. The terms need three things: when payment is due, how to pay, and what reference to quote. The due date can be stated as a date — "Payment due by 7 December 2026" — or as a period from the invoice date — "Payment due within 14 days of the invoice date" — and stating both, as in the worked example, is the clearest approach. The payment method should be the method you agreed with the client: bank transfer, a payment link, or another method, with the details the client needs to make the payment. Check the bank details, the account name, and any payment link carefully before you send the invoice, because a wrong digit delays payment and creates work for both sides. The reference should be the invoice number, so the payment can be matched to the invoice without a query. Our guide to invoice payment terms explains the common terms — Net 14, Net 30, due on receipt, and deposits — and how to choose and word them, including late-payment wording and what to do when a client asks for longer terms.

Keep the terms consistent with the agreement. If the quote said Net 14, the invoice should say Net 14, not Net 30. If the client asked for a deposit, the terms should state how much was due before work started and how much is due now. If you charge a late-payment fee or interest, state it in the terms, and check that the wording is lawful where you are based before you rely on it. Do not add terms the client has not seen before on the invoice itself: new terms introduced on an invoice, after the work is done, are likely to be disputed, and they damage the relationship. Agree the terms before the work starts, put them in the quote or the contract, and repeat them on the invoice. If the client needs the invoice in a particular format — a PDF, a particular file name, or a portal upload — follow their format, and keep a copy of what you sent. The format is part of the terms, in practice, because an invoice in the wrong format will not be processed.

Step 8: Check, Save, and Send

Before you send the invoice, check it as if you were the client's finance team. Read it once for accuracy: the names, the addresses, the number, the date, the description, the quantities, the prices, the totals, and the bank details. Read it once for agreement: does it match the quote, the purchase order, and the terms you agreed? Read it once for completeness: is every required field present, including any tax number or reference your jurisdiction or the client requires? One careful read, taking two minutes, prevents most of the queries that delay payment. Then save a copy — a PDF, with a clear file name that includes the invoice number and the client name, such as INV-014-Greenfield-Bakery.pdf — and keep it in a folder with the quote, the agreement, and the record of payment. That folder is your audit trail, and it is what you, your accountant, or a tax authority will want to see if the invoice is ever questioned.

Send the invoice promptly, on the date you dated it, by the method the client prefers. For most freelancers, that means email, with the invoice attached as a PDF and the key details in the body of the email: the invoice number, the amount, the due date, and a polite line asking the client to confirm receipt. Our guide on how to send an invoice by email covers the subject line, the message, and the attachment, so the invoice is opened and acted on rather than missed. If the client uses a portal, upload the invoice there, and send a short email as well, so the client knows it is waiting. After sending, note the date you sent it, and diary the due date. If payment has not arrived by the due date, follow up politely in writing, with the invoice number, the amount, and the due date, and keep a record of every follow-up. Our guide to chasing overdue invoices covers the follow-up steps, from a polite reminder to a firmer letter, and when to seek advice. And if the client is a repeat client, consider whether a recurring invoice system would suit the work: for a fixed monthly fee, a recurring invoice saves the writing time altogether, and the steps in this guide become the template the recurring system follows each month.

Common Mistakes When Writing an Invoice

Most invoice mistakes are small, and all of them are avoidable. Reusing or skipping a number. A reused number makes two invoices indistinguishable, and a skipped number raises a question from an accountant or a tax authority. Keep one sequence, and check the last number before you start. Leaving out the client's reference. A purchase order number or cost code that the client needs is not a detail. It is the key to their payment system, and leaving it off delays payment. Writing a vague description. "Work done" or "services as agreed" cannot be approved by a finance team that did not see the work. Write the description for a stranger. Getting the totals wrong. A total that does not add up, a tax line calculated on the wrong base, or a deposit not credited will be queried, and the query will cost more time than the checking would have. Forgetting the due date or the payment method. An invoice that does not say when or how to pay will be paid when the client gets round to it, which is usually later than you need. Sending the invoice late. A week late in sending is usually a week late in being paid, because the client's payment cycle starts when the invoice arrives. Changing the terms on the invoice. New terms introduced after the work is done are likely to be disputed. Agree the terms first, and repeat them on the invoice. Not keeping a copy. An invoice sent and not saved is an invoice you cannot prove, cannot chase, and cannot show your accountant. Save every invoice, with its quote and its payment record, in one folder per client.

After the Invoice: Records, Corrections, and Repeats

An invoice is not finished when it is sent. It is finished when it is paid, recorded, and filed. When payment arrives, match it to the invoice number, note the date and the amount, and keep the payment record with the invoice. If the payment is short, or if it arrives without a reference, contact the client promptly and reconcile the difference in writing, rather than letting an unexplained shortfall sit in your records. If you discover an error in an invoice after it has been sent — a wrong amount, a wrong description, or a wrong tax figure — do not edit the original and resend it as if nothing happened. Issue a credit note that cancels the original, or a corrected invoice that refers to the original number, and explain the change in a covering message. The audit trail matters: an invoice sequence with a corrected document that explains itself is clean, and a sequence with a silently altered document is not. Keep the original, the correction, and the payment record together, so the story of the sale can be followed from quote to payment without a gap.

For repeat work, turn a good invoice into a template. Save a copy with the client's details, the usual description format, and the agreed terms, and use it as the starting point for the next invoice. Check the number, the date, the description, and the totals each time — a template is a starting point, not a document to send unchecked — but the layout, the wording, and the payment details will already be right. Over time, that habit turns invoicing from a chore that takes an evening into a routine that takes minutes. And that routine is the point of this guide: an invoice is not a test of your writing, and it is not a negotiation. It is a clear, complete record of work done, money owed, and terms agreed, written in the same order every time, checked before it is sent, and filed when it is paid. Write it that way, and the invoice will do its job quietly, while you get on with the work you were hired to do.

Frequently asked questions

What must be on every freelance invoice?

Every invoice needs the word Invoice, a unique invoice number, the date, your business name and contact details, the client's name and address, a clear description of the work with quantities and prices, the subtotal, any tax or discount, the total due, the payment terms and due date, and how to pay. If your jurisdiction requires extra details, such as a tax number, add those too. A missing field is the most common reason an invoice is delayed by a client's finance team, so check the list before you send it.

How do I number my first invoice?

Start at 001 or 0001 and count up by one for every invoice, in the order you issue them, with no gaps, no duplicates, and no reuse. Keep one sequence for all clients. Do not restart the sequence for each client, and do not renumber an invoice after it has been sent. If you need to correct a sent invoice, issue a credit note or a corrected invoice that refers to the original number. Our guide to invoice numbering explains the rules in detail.

When should I send an invoice as a freelancer?

Send it as soon as the work is complete, or on the date the agreement says — for example, on completion, weekly, or monthly for ongoing work. Invoicing promptly matters: the sooner the client receives the invoice, the sooner their payment process starts, and a delay of a week in sending is usually a delay of a week in being paid. If the agreement includes a deposit or an advance, the deposit is usually billed before work starts, and the balance on completion. Agree the timing in writing before the work starts, so the invoice date is never a surprise.

Should I use an invoice template or an invoice generator?

Either can work, and the choice is about accuracy and time. A template is a good start for your first few invoices, because you can see every field and learn what goes where. A generator is better once you invoice regularly, because it calculates the totals, keeps the numbering straight, and produces a consistent layout every time. Whichever you use, check the finished invoice field by field before sending it.
