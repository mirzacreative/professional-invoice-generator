How to Invoice International Clients: Currency & Tax

Updated 27 November 2026 · 16-minute read

Quick answer: Invoicing an international client is an ordinary invoice with four extra decisions made in advance: the currency, the exchange-rate method, the tax treatment, and who bears the payment charges. Agree all four in writing, in the quote or contract, before the work starts. Invoice in the agreed currency, state the currency on every amount, show the tax treatment in the wording your jurisdiction requires, state the payment method and the due date plainly, and keep the quote, the invoice, the payment record, and any conversion evidence together as one file. This guide explains each decision in general terms, with an illustrative worked example whose figures are invented and labelled as such. It is general information only, not legal or tax advice. The rules on currency, tax registration, and the taxation of cross-border supplies vary by jurisdiction, and the rules for a particular invoice must be checked where you are based, and where the client is based, with a qualified adviser where the question is specific. The guides linked throughout cover the steps in depth: quote versus invoice, proforma invoices, invoicing as a sole trader, how to write an invoice, payment terms, and hourly versus project pricing. Use the free invoice generator to produce the invoice itself once the decisions in this guide have been made.

An international invoice differs from a domestic invoice in a way that is easy to underestimate, because the difference is not in the layout. The layout is the same: a number, a date, two parties, a description, amounts, a total, and terms. The difference is in the number of systems the invoice has to satisfy at once. A domestic invoice lives inside one currency, one tax system, one set of banking arrangements, and one time zone. An international invoice lives across two or more of each, and every point at which the two systems meet is a point at which something can be misunderstood: the currency in which the price was quoted, the rate at which it converts, the tax that does or does not apply, the charges deducted on the way, the number of days a payment takes to arrive, and the law that governs a dispute if one arises. None of those points is difficult on its own. Together, they are the reason international invoicing feels uncertain, and the reason the discipline in this guide is to settle each point before the work starts, in writing, rather than to settle it on the invoice, after the work is done, when the client has the work and the freelancer has only the invoice.

The most useful way to think about an international invoice is as the last document in a chain, not as the first. The chain begins with the enquiry, continues through the quote, the acceptance, and the delivery, and ends with the invoice and the payment. Every decision that matters for the invoice should already have been made by the time the invoice is written, and the invoice should contain no surprise. A client who agreed a price in one currency and receives an invoice in another, a client who agreed a fixed price and receives an invoice with a tax line that was never mentioned, and a client who agreed to pay by one method and receives payment details for another, will each query the invoice, and each query will delay payment by days or weeks, across time zones, in a correspondence that is harder to conduct than a domestic exchange. The discipline is therefore not a matter of invoice design. It is a matter of agreement, made early, recorded plainly, and followed exactly. This guide takes the decisions in the order in which they should be made: first the commercial agreement, then the currency, then the tax treatment, then the payment method, and finally the invoice itself and the record that supports it.

1. Before the First Invoice: The Agreement That Prevents Most Disputes

Before any work starts for an international client, put the commercial terms in writing, in a quote or a written confirmation that the client accepts, and keep that acceptance with the job file. The terms that matter most for an international job are the ones that a domestic job can leave unsaid, and they are worth listing explicitly, because each of them is a dispute waiting to happen if it is left to assumption. State the scope of the work, the deliverables, and the dates, as any quote should. State the price, and state the currency in which the price is fixed, in words and in figures, so that there is no room for a later argument about which currency was meant. State whether the price includes or excludes any tax, and in what circumstances the tax treatment might change, in the general terms explained in section 4, without attempting to settle the tax position in the quote itself. State the payment method, who bears the charges, and the due date, counted from a stated event — the invoice date, the delivery date, or the acceptance date — rather than from a vague phrase such as "on completion". State the client's full name and address, including the country, and the name of the person or department the invoice should be addressed to, because a cross-border invoice addressed only to a business name, with no address, will be difficult for the client to process and difficult for you to support in your own records.

The agreement should also settle the points that only arise across borders. State the language of the invoice and of the supporting documents, where the client and the freelancer do not share a first language, and state which version governs if documents exist in two languages. State the time zone in which deadlines and due dates are counted, because a due date of "Friday" means a different working day in different time zones, and a payment sent on a Friday in one country may not arrive until the following week in another. State how revisions, extra work, and changes of scope will be charged, and in which currency, because extra work agreed informally in a message, without a price, is the most common source of an invoice that a client disputes. And state, in plain terms, what happens if payment is late, by reference to the payment-terms guide, so that a reminder is the enforcement of an agreement rather than the opening of a negotiation. None of this requires a lawyer for an ordinary freelance job, and none of it should be presented as legal advice. It requires a written record, made before the work, that both sides have seen.

A final point about the agreement concerns the client's identity and status. For a domestic client, the freelancer usually knows, from the enquiry and the conversation, whether the client is a business or an individual, and whether the client is registered for tax. For an international client, that knowledge is harder to obtain and easier to assume wrongly. Ask, plainly, whether the client is a business or an individual, ask for the full legal name and address, and, where the tax treatment depends on it, ask for any tax registration number the client holds and that the local rules require you to record. A client who will not supply a full name and address is a client whose invoice cannot be completed properly, and whose payment cannot be properly recorded, and the time to discover that is before the work, not after it. Keep the client's answers with the quote, exactly as given, and do not guess a client's status from a website, an email address, or a social-media profile. The invoice should reflect the client's status as the client has stated it, and the evidence for that status should be in the file.

2. Choosing the Currency: Whose Convenience, Whose Risk

The currency decision is a decision about who bears the risk of movement between the quote and the payment. If the freelancer invoices in the client's currency, the client knows exactly what it will pay, in money it understands, and the freelancer bears the risk that the amount, when converted, is worth less in the freelancer's own currency than it was worth when the work was quoted. If the freelancer invoices in its own currency, the freelancer knows exactly what it will receive, in money it can use directly, and the client bears the risk that the amount, in the client's currency, rises between the quote and the payment. If the freelancer invoices in a third currency, such as the United States dollar, which is commonly used as a neutral currency in international trade, both sides bear a conversion, and the arrangement is convenient only if both sides are comfortable with it. There is no universally right choice, and the right choice for a particular job is the choice both sides agree to, in writing, before the work starts, with the consequences understood.

Several practical considerations should inform the choice. The first is the client's payment system: a client whose accounts are run in one currency will pay more readily, and with fewer queries, in that currency, and a freelancer who insists on another currency may find that the client's payment process adds delay, conversion, and internal approval. The second is the freelancer's own costs: a freelancer whose rent, tax, and living costs are in one currency will prefer to be paid in that currency, or to convert promptly on receipt, rather than to hold a balance in another currency and convert it later at an unknown rate. The third is the size and duration of the job: for a small, one-off job, the conversion cost may be a substantial proportion of the fee, and the choice of currency and payment method should be made with that cost in mind; for a large or long-running job, the movement of the exchange rate over the life of the job may matter more than the conversion cost on any single payment, and the agreement should address whether the price is fixed for the whole job or reviewed at stated points. The fourth is the freelancer's banking and payment arrangements: some payment methods hold balances in several currencies and convert at a stated rate, while others convert at the point of payment at a rate the freelancer cannot see in advance, and the choice of currency should be made with the actual payment route in mind, not in the abstract.

Whatever currency is chosen, the invoice must state it plainly, on every amount, and in one currency only. Do not invoice in two currencies at once, do not show a total in one currency and a tax line in another, and do not leave the currency to be inferred from a symbol, because a symbol such as "$" is used by many currencies, and an invoice that shows "$1,000" without stating which dollar it means is an invoice that invites a query. Write the currency code, such as USD, EUR, or GBP, alongside the amounts, and write it in the total line in full, so that the amount due is unambiguous. If a conversion is shown on the invoice for the client's information, label it clearly as a conversion, state the rate and the date used, and make clear which amount is the amount due and which amount is the converted figure for information only. A client should never have to guess which figure to pay, and a freelancer should never have to guess which figure the client paid. The invoice should answer that question on its face, and the worked example in section 6 shows how the answer should look.

3. Exchange Rates: Timing, Source, and the Record

Exchange rates move, and the movement between the quote, the invoice, and the payment is a real commercial fact that an international invoice must handle, rather than ignore. The first question is when the rate is fixed. In a typical arrangement, the price is fixed in the invoice currency at the quote stage, and the rate matters only when the amount is converted for the freelancer's own records, for tax reporting, or for the client's internal accounting. In another arrangement, the price is quoted in one currency and invoiced in another, and the rate used for the conversion must be agreed, or the conversion will be disputed. In a third arrangement, the price is quoted in the freelancer's currency, invoiced in the client's currency at a rate agreed at the invoice date, and the client pays the converted amount. Each arrangement is workable, and each requires the rate, the date, and the source to be stated, so that the conversion can be checked. The arrangement that causes disputes is the one in which no rate was agreed, no date was stated, and each side converts at a different rate, on a different day, from a different source, and arrives at a different figure.

The source of the rate matters as much as the rate itself. A rate taken from a bank, a payment provider, a central bank, or a published reference rate will differ, on the same day, from a rate taken from a search engine, a news report, or a currency-converter website, because the rates include different margins, are quoted at different times of day, and may be buying rates, selling rates, or mid-market rates. The agreement should therefore state the source, or the invoice should state it, and the same source should be used consistently for the whole job, rather than a source chosen after the event because it gives a favourable figure. Record the rate, the source, the date, and the time where the source states one, and keep that record with the invoice, so that the conversion can be reproduced later, for the freelancer's own accounts, for a tax return, or for a client's query. A conversion that cannot be reproduced is a conversion that cannot be defended, and a freelancer who converts at a rate that cannot be shown will find the figure questioned, however honestly it was chosen.

Two further points about timing deserve attention. The first is the gap between the invoice date and the payment date. A payment that is due in thirty days, and that is paid on the thirtieth day, may arrive at a rate quite different from the rate on the invoice date, and the freelancer who has fixed the price in the client's currency will receive a different amount in its own currency from the amount it expected. That is the risk the freelancer accepted when it chose the invoice currency, and it should be accepted consciously, with the price set to allow for ordinary movement, rather than discovered afterwards as an unpleasant surprise. The second point is the treatment of the conversion in the freelancer's own records. Tax and accounting rules commonly require income to be reported in the freelancer's own currency, converted at a rate determined by rules that vary by jurisdiction, and the conversion used for reporting may not be the conversion used for the invoice. Keep the two conversions separate: the invoice conversion, which decides what the client pays, and the reporting conversion, which decides what the freelancer reports. Record both, label both, and do not confuse one with the other. For the reporting conversion, check the rules where you are based, and ask a qualified adviser, because this guide states the question, not the answer for any particular jurisdiction.

4. Tax Notes: General Terms Only, and the Questions to Ask

Tax is the part of international invoicing where general guidance reaches its limit most quickly, and where a specific question must be answered by the rules of the relevant jurisdictions and, where needed, by a qualified adviser. This section therefore states the questions to ask, the notes to show, and the evidence to keep, in general terms, and it does not state the tax position for any country, any type of supply, or any particular invoice. The questions are the same questions the sole-trader guide raises, with the cross-border element added, and they should be asked before the first international invoice, not after it. Are you registered for the relevant tax, such as value added tax or a sales tax, in the jurisdiction where you are based? Does your registration, or the scale of your turnover, require you to charge tax on supplies to clients in other countries? Does the tax treatment change if the client is a business rather than an individual, if the client is registered for tax in its own jurisdiction, or if the supply is of a particular type, such as a digital service, a service supplied in person, or a supply of goods? Does the treatment change if the client is in a particular country, or in a particular grouping of countries, such as the European Union? And what evidence must you keep — the client's address, the client's status, the nature of the supply, and the rule relied on — to support the treatment shown on the invoice?

Each of those questions has a different answer in different jurisdictions, and an answer copied from another freelancer, or from a guide written for another country, may be wrong for you. The concept most often encountered in cross-border services is the place of supply: the rule that decides where, for tax purposes, a service is treated as supplied, and therefore which jurisdiction's tax rules apply to it. In many systems, the place of supply for a service to a business client in another country is the client's country, and the treatment may involve the client accounting for the tax itself, under an arrangement commonly called the reverse charge. In other systems, or for other types of supply, the place of supply may be the supplier's country, and the supplier may be required to charge tax as it would for a domestic supply. This guide states those concepts only as concepts to be checked, because the detail — which supplies, which clients, which countries, and which wording — varies, and a freelancer who applies a concept without checking the detail for its own jurisdiction may charge tax that should not be charged, or omit tax that should have been charged, and may create a problem for both itself and the client. Do not copy a tax line, a tax note, or a tax rate from another freelancer's invoice. Check the rules where you are based, and ask a qualified tax adviser before you charge or stop charging tax on a cross-border invoice.

On the face of the invoice, the tax treatment should be shown in a way that the client can check and that a tax authority can follow. A trader who is registered and who must charge tax commonly shows the subtotal, the tax at the applicable rate, shown separately, with the rate and the registration number, and the total including tax, as the sole-trader guide explains. A trader who is not registered, or whose supply is treated as not subject to tax in its jurisdiction for a stated reason, commonly shows a total with no tax line, and, where the local practice or the client's requirements demand it, a short note stating the position, in the wording the local rules prescribe or that local practice accepts. A trader who supplies to a client in another country should show the treatment that applies to that supply, should state the client's country and status where the rules require it, and should keep the evidence — the client's address, the nature of the supply, and the rule relied on — with the invoice. In every case, the invoice should not contain a tax statement that the freelancer cannot support, and it should not contain a tax statement in wording borrowed from another jurisdiction. If the tax position is uncertain, the correct course is to ask an adviser before issuing the invoice, not to issue the invoice and hope the position is not questioned. An invoice that charges tax incorrectly, or that omits tax that should have been charged, can create a problem for the client as well as for the freelancer, because the client's own tax records will reflect the treatment shown on the invoice.

A related question concerns registration thresholds and the scale of international work. A freelancer whose domestic turnover is below a registration threshold may find that international supplies are treated differently for threshold purposes, or that a particular type of supply requires registration regardless of turnover, or that registration in another jurisdiction is required once supplies to clients there pass a threshold. These are questions for the rules of the relevant jurisdictions, and they should be asked when international work begins, or when it grows beyond an occasional job, rather than at the end of a tax year when the position can no longer be changed for the invoices already issued. Keep a record of the countries supplied to, the types of supply, and the amounts, so that the questions can be answered from evidence rather than from memory. A freelancer who keeps that record, and who asks the questions early, will find the tax treatment of international work manageable. A freelancer who does not keep the record will find the same questions difficult to answer, and the answers, when they are eventually given, harder to support.

5. Getting Paid Across Borders: Methods, Charges, and Timing

The payment method is a commercial decision as much as a practical one, and it should be agreed before the work starts, for the same reason as the currency: a client who is asked to pay by a method it does not use, or cannot use, will delay, and a freelancer who accepts a method without understanding its charges will receive less than the invoiced amount. The methods commonly available to a freelancer include a bank transfer, a payment provider that holds balances in several currencies, and a payment link or platform provided by an invoicing or marketplace service. Each method has a different cost, a different speed, and a different record, and the choice should be made by comparing those three things for the particular job, rather than by habit. A bank transfer may be slow and may pass through intermediary banks that deduct charges, but it may be the method the client's accounts department can process most easily. A payment provider may be fast and may show the charges plainly, but it may require the client to hold an account with the same provider, or it may convert the payment at a rate the freelancer cannot see in advance. A payment link may be convenient for a small job, but it may carry a percentage fee that is a substantial part of a small fee, and it may hold the funds for a period before they can be withdrawn.

Charges deserve the same explicit agreement as the currency. Bank charges, intermediary-bank charges, conversion spreads, and provider fees can all reduce the amount that arrives, and the agreement should state who bears them. The clearest arrangement, from the freelancer's point of view, is that the client pays all charges, so that the invoiced amount arrives in full, and the client should be told that requirement before the work starts, so that it can choose a payment route that achieves it. The next clearest arrangement is that charges are shared in a stated way, or that the freelancer accepts the deduction and sets the price to allow for it. The arrangement that causes disputes is the one in which nothing was said, the client paid the invoiced amount by a route that deducted charges, and the freelancer treats the shortfall as an underpayment. State the arrangement in the quote, repeat it on the invoice in the payment details, and check the amount that actually arrives against the amount invoiced, keeping the bank or provider record with the invoice. If the amount that arrives is less than the invoiced amount, the record will show whether the difference is a charge, a conversion difference, or a genuine shortfall, and the freelancer can then raise the right question with the client, with the evidence in hand.

Timing is the third part of the payment decision, and it is the part most affected by time zones and by bank holidays. A payment sent on a Friday in the client's country, where Friday is a working day, may not arrive until the following week in the freelancer's country, where the weekend or a holiday intervenes. A payment sent through a method that takes three to five working days will arrive later than a payment sent through a method that arrives the same day, and a due date counted in calendar days will mean something different from a due date counted in working days. The agreement should therefore state the due date in a way that both sides can count: a calendar date, a number of days from a stated event, and the time zone in which the date is counted. The invoice should repeat the due date, the payment method, and the details the client needs to pay, checked before the invoice is sent, because a wrong account number, a wrong reference, or a payment detail that has changed is a payment that goes to the wrong place or that cannot be matched to the invoice. A change of bank or payment details should be told to clients in advance, in a separate message, not only on the next invoice, and a client who receives a sudden change of payment details on an invoice alone should be expected to query it, because payment-detail fraud is a real risk, and a careful client is a good client. The guide to sending an invoice by email covers the covering message that should accompany the invoice, including the payment details and the reference to quote, and the same discipline applies with extra force across borders.

Finally, the freelancer should decide, before the work starts, how much work will be done before payment, and on what terms. For a domestic client, the risk of non-payment is real, and for an international client, the risk is greater, because the remedies — a reminder, a debt-collection process, a legal claim — are harder to pursue across borders, and the cost of pursuing them may exceed the amount owed. The practical protections are the same as for any freelance job, applied more strictly: a written agreement, a deposit or staged payments for a large job, a pause on further work if a payment is overdue, and a record of every delivery and every message. Staged payments, in particular, suit international work well, because they limit the amount at risk at any point, and they give the client a reason to pay promptly at each stage, in order to receive the next stage. The payment-terms guide explains deposits, staged payments, and late-payment terms in detail, and the recurring-invoices guide covers the case of regular international work, where the same terms repeat each month. Do not extend credit to an international client on the strength of a friendly correspondence, and do not start a second job for a client whose first invoice is unpaid, until the position is clear.

6. Worked Example: One International Invoice, Step by Step (Illustrative Figures)

The worked example below uses invented, illustrative figures only. They are not a recommended price, not a tax rate, and not an exchange rate. They exist to show the arithmetic and the layout of an international invoice, and the way the currency, the conversion, and the tax note appear on the face of the document. Every figure in the example is labelled illustrative, and the example should not be copied as a template for a real invoice without checking the currency, the tax treatment, and the payment details for the particular job.

The example is a freelancer based in one country, invoicing a business client in another country, for a fixed-price design job. The price was agreed in the quote as 800 units of the client's currency, with the tax treatment stated in the general terms of the quote and confirmed with an adviser before the invoice was issued. The invoice is issued in the client's currency, so that the client pays the amount it agreed, and the freelancer will convert the receipt for its own records at a stated rate from a stated source. The illustrative figures are as follows: the agreed price is 800, the tax charged on the invoice is nil, because the treatment confirmed for this illustrative supply is that no tax is charged on the face of the invoice, with a short note stating the position in the wording the freelancer's jurisdiction requires, and the total due is 800. The illustrative conversion, for the freelancer's own records only, uses an invented rate of 1 unit of the client's currency to 0.85 units of the freelancer's currency, taken from an invented source on the invoice date, giving an illustrative converted figure of 680 units of the freelancer's currency. That converted figure is not the amount due, it is not shown as the total on the invoice, and it is recorded separately, with the rate, the source, and the date, in the freelancer's own file.

The invoice itself, in the example, shows the following fields, in order: the freelancer's name, trading name, address, and contact details; the invoice number, in the unbroken sequence explained in the invoice-numbering guide; the invoice date, and the date or period of supply, where that differs; the client's full legal name, address, and country, and the name of the person or department the invoice is addressed to; a description of the supply, naming the deliverable, the date or period it relates to, and the basis of the charge — in this example, a fixed price for a named deliverable, as the hourly-versus-project guide explains; the line total, the subtotal, the tax line, shown as nil with the short note, and the total due, all in the client's currency, with the currency code stated on every amount; the payment terms, including the due date, counted from the invoice date, and the payment method, with the account or provider details checked before sending; and the references, including the quote reference and any order reference the client supplied. The covering email, following the email guide, states the invoice number, the total, the currency, the due date, and the payment method, and asks the client to quote the invoice number when paying, so that the payment can be matched without a query.

The record that supports the example invoice is as important as the invoice itself, and it consists of the quote, with the currency and the tax treatment stated in general terms, the client's written acceptance, the delivery record, showing what was delivered and when, the invoice, the payment record, showing the amount that arrived, the date, and the method, and the conversion record, showing the illustrative rate, source, and date used for the freelancer's own reporting. If the client queries the invoice, the record answers the query. If a tax authority asks about the treatment, the record supports it. If the payment arrives short, the record shows whether the difference is a charge, a conversion difference, or a shortfall. The example is a small job, and the record is a small file, but the habit scales: the same file, kept for every international job, is the habit that makes international invoicing manageable, and the absence of that file is the reason international invoicing feels unmanageable to a freelancer who keeps only the invoice.

7. Late Payment Across Borders: Patience, Evidence, and the Right Reminder

Late payment from an international client has more innocent explanations than late payment from a domestic client, and the freelancer should allow for them before assuming the worst. A payment may be late because it was sent on a bank holiday in the client's country, because it is held for a compliance check, because it was sent by a method that takes several working days, because the client's accounts department works in a different time zone and processes payments on a weekly cycle, or because the invoice was addressed to the wrong person or department and has waited in a queue. Each of those explanations is common, each is innocent, and each is resolved by a polite reminder that states the facts and asks a question, rather than by an accusation. The reminder should state the invoice number, the amount, the currency, the due date, and the payment method, and it should ask the client to confirm that the payment has been sent, by which method, and on what date, so that the freelancer can look for it and can distinguish a payment that is in transit from a payment that has not been sent. The late-payment guide explains the reminder sequence in detail, and the same sequence applies internationally, with the extra allowance for transit time and for time-zone delay built into the intervals between reminders.

If the payment is genuinely overdue under the agreed terms, and the reminders have not produced it, the freelancer should follow the terms agreed before the work started, keep a record of every message, and pause further work on credit until the position is clear. The decision about further steps — a formal demand, a collection process, or a legal claim — depends on the amount, the jurisdiction, and the cost of pursuing it, and it is a decision on which advice should be taken, because the remedies available in the client's jurisdiction, and the cost of using them, vary widely, and a step taken without advice may cost more than the amount owed. This guide does not give legal advice, and it does not recommend a course of action for a disputed or unpaid international invoice beyond the reminder, the record, and the advice. What it does recommend is prevention: a deposit or staged payments for a large job, a written agreement, and a pause on credit, which together make the late-payment problem smaller, rarer, and easier to resolve. A freelancer who has followed those steps, and who is still unpaid, is in a far stronger position, practically and evidentially, than a freelancer who worked without an agreement and is now trying to reconstruct the terms from messages.

One further cause of apparent late payment deserves a mention, because it is specific to international work and is often mistaken for non-payment. A payment may have been sent, in full, in the agreed currency, and may have arrived short, because charges were deducted on the way, or because the payment was converted at a rate the freelancer did not expect. The freelancer who treats the shortfall as a late or partial payment, without checking the bank record, will raise the wrong question with the client, and the client, who paid in full, will be puzzled and defensive. Check the amount that arrived, check the charges and the conversion shown on the bank or provider record, and compare the arrival with the agreement about who bears the charges. If the agreement was that the client bears all charges, the shortfall is a matter to raise, with the record, politely. If the agreement was that the freelancer bears the charges, or if nothing was agreed, the shortfall is the cost of the arrangement, and the lesson is to agree the charges explicitly next time. In either case, the bank record, kept with the invoice, is the evidence that decides the question, and it should be consulted before any message is sent.

8. Language, Addresses, and the Details That Delay Payment

International invoices fail, in practice, more often on details than on principles. An address that is incomplete, a name that is misspelled, a reference that is missing, or a description that the client's accounts department cannot match to an approval, will delay a payment by days, and the delay will be harder to resolve across time zones than it would be domestically. The discipline is to check the details against the quote and the client's own documents, and to show them exactly as the client has given them. Use the client's full legal name, not a shortened or familiar form, and use the full postal address, including the country, in the form the client's country uses, because an address that is correct in the freelancer's country may be incomplete or misordered in the client's. Show the client's tax registration number, where the rules require it or where the client has supplied it for that purpose, and show the freelancer's own registration number, where the freelancer is registered and the rules require it. Show the reference the client supplied — an order number, a project code, or a cost-centre code — exactly as supplied, because that reference is the key by which the client's system matches the invoice to the approval, and an invoice without it may wait in a queue until someone asks for it.

Language deserves the same care. An invoice in the freelancer's language, sent to a client whose accounts department works in another language, may be processed slowly, or may be returned for a translation, and the delay will be attributed to the invoice rather than to the language. Where the client and the freelancer do not share a first language, agree the language of the invoice in advance, and, where the invoice must be in a language the freelancer does not write fluently, have the wording checked, particularly the tax note, the payment terms, and the description, because a mistranslation in those fields can change the meaning of the document. Keep the description in plain words, with dates and quantities, so that it can be understood by a person who did not commission the work, because the person who processes the payment is commonly not the person who commissioned it, and the description is the document that explains the charge to that person. A description that says only "services" or "work done" is not clear enough for a domestic invoice, and it is less clear still for an international one, where the reader may be working in a second language and under time pressure.

9. Record-Keeping for International Work

Record-keeping for international work follows the same principles as for domestic work, with the cross-border evidence added. Keep every invoice issued and received, with the quote, the acceptance, the delivery record, the payment record, and any credit note, for the period the jurisdiction where you are based requires for tax and accounting records, which is commonly several years. The exact period varies, so check the rule where you are based rather than relying on a general figure. Keep the records in a form that can be produced on request, with the invoice numbers in one unbroken sequence, and keep a backup of electronic records. For each international job, keep, in addition, the evidence that supports the tax treatment — the client's address and country, the client's status as stated by the client, the nature of the supply, and the rule relied on — the currency agreement, the conversion record, with the rate, the source, and the date, and the payment record, showing the amount that arrived, the charges deducted, and the method used. That file is the evidence that answers a client's query, supports a tax return, and proves, years later, what was agreed and what was paid.

Organisation matters, because international work accumulates records in several currencies, several time zones, and sometimes several languages, and a record that cannot be found is a record that does not exist for practical purposes. Keep one file per job, per client, named by the invoice number, containing the quote, the acceptance, the delivery record, the invoice, the conversion record, and the payment record, in that order, and keep a single list of invoices, in sequence, showing the client, the country, the currency, the amount, the tax treatment in general terms, and the payment status. That list is the document that shows, at a glance, which international invoices are unpaid, which are in transit, and which have arrived short, and it is the document that should be checked weekly, so that a late payment is noticed in days rather than in months. The numbering guide and the recurring-invoices guide explain the numbering and the repeat-invoicing discipline that the list depends on. A freelancer who keeps that list, and who checks it weekly, will find international invoicing a routine task. A freelancer who does not will find it a source of recurring surprise.

10. Checklist: The International Invoice, From Quote to Payment

  1. Agree in writing before the work: scope, price, currency, tax treatment in general terms, payment method, who bears the charges, due date, and time zone.
  2. Record the client's identity: full legal name, full address including country, status as stated by the client, and any tax registration number the rules require.
  3. Check the tax position: registration, place of supply, client status, and type of supply, under the rules where you are based, with a qualified adviser where the question is specific. Do not copy another invoice's tax treatment.
  4. Invoice in one currency: state the currency code on every amount, show one total due, and label any conversion as information only, with the rate, source, and date.
  5. Show the full invoice fields: unique number, date, supply date where relevant, both parties' details, a clear description, amounts that add up, tax shown separately where it applies, total due, and payment terms.
  6. State the payment details plainly: method, account or provider details checked before sending, reference to quote, and due date counted from a stated event.
  7. Send with a covering message: invoice number, total, currency, due date, and payment method, following the email guide, and ask the client to quote the invoice number when paying.
  8. Keep the file together: quote, acceptance, delivery record, invoice, conversion record, and payment record, in one file per job, named by invoice number.
  9. Check the arrival: compare the amount that arrives with the amount invoiced, using the bank or provider record, before raising any question with the client.
  10. Follow up on time: allow for transit and time zones, remind politely with the facts, pause further work on credit if payment is genuinely overdue, and take advice before further steps on a disputed or unpaid invoice.

International invoicing, done in this order, is not a difficult task. It is an ordinary invoice, preceded by an agreement, followed by a record, and carried out with the care that two systems, two currencies, and two time zones require. The freelancer who agrees the currency, the tax treatment, and the charges before the work, who invoices in the agreed currency with the treatment shown plainly, who checks the payment details before sending, and who keeps the file together, will be paid promptly by most international clients, most of the time, and will be able to answer the queries that do arise from evidence rather than from memory. The freelancer who skips the agreement, who invoices in a currency never agreed, who copies a tax treatment from another invoice, and who keeps only the invoice, will find international work a source of delay and dispute, and will attribute the difficulty to the clients, when the difficulty was in the process. The choice is in the process, and the process is in the freelancer's control. Begin with the agreement, and let the invoice follow it. Frequently asked questions

Which currency should I invoice an international client in?

There is no single correct currency. Agree the currency in writing before the work starts, in the quote or contract, and invoice in that currency. Many freelancers invoice in the client's currency for the client's convenience, or in their own currency to keep their income predictable, and either choice is workable if it is agreed in advance. What causes disputes is not the choice itself but a currency that was never agreed, or a currency changed on the invoice without warning. State the currency plainly on the invoice, on every amount, so that neither side has to guess.

What exchange rate should I use on an international invoice?

The rate to use depends on why the conversion is needed, and the answer is a matter for the tax and accounting rules where you are based, so check those rules or ask a qualified adviser. In general terms, the invoice itself should be in one currency, and any conversion for your own records should use a stated, dated rate from a stated source, kept with the invoice. If the amount must be converted — for example, to report income in your own currency — record the rate, the source, and the date used, and apply the same method consistently, rather than choosing a favourable rate after the event.

Do I charge tax or VAT on an invoice to a client abroad?

It depends on the jurisdiction you are based in, the jurisdiction of the client, the type of supply, and whether you are registered for the relevant tax, so no general guide can state the answer for a particular invoice. In many systems, the tax treatment of a cross-border supply of services differs from the treatment of a domestic supply, and the difference turns on rules such as the place of supply, the client's status, and whether the supply is exported. Show the tax treatment you have applied, in the wording your jurisdiction requires, and keep the evidence that supports it. For a specific invoice, ask a qualified tax adviser before you charge tax, omit tax, or copy a tax treatment from another invoice.

Who pays the bank and conversion fees on an international payment?

Whoever the agreement says pays them, which is why the agreement should say so before the work starts. Bank charges, intermediary-bank charges, and conversion spreads can reduce the amount that arrives, and a client who pays in full but through a route that deducts charges has not, from the freelancer's point of view, paid in full. Agree in writing whether the client pays all charges so that the invoiced amount arrives in full, whether charges are shared, or whether the freelancer accepts the deduction. State the payment method on the invoice, and check the amount that actually arrives against the amount invoiced, keeping the bank record with the invoice.

What should I do if an international client pays late?

Start with the same steps as for a domestic late payment, but allow for the extra causes that cross-border payments have: time-zone delay, a bank holiday in the client's country, a payment held for a compliance check, or a payment sent by a method that takes several working days. Send a polite reminder that states the invoice number, the amount, the currency, and the due date, and ask the client to confirm that the payment has been sent and by which method. If the payment is genuinely overdue under the agreed terms, follow the reminder sequence in the payment-terms guide, keep a record of every message, and do not start new work on credit until the position is clear. For a disputed amount or a large unpaid invoice, take advice on the options in the client's jurisdiction before taking further steps.
