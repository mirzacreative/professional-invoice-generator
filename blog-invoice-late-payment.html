Invoice Late Payment: How to Chase Overdue Invoices

Updated 18 November 2026 · 9-minute read

Quick answer: Chase an overdue invoice in three stages: a friendly nudge a few days after the due date, a firmer reminder at about two weeks overdue, and a final notice at 30 days. Each message should restate the invoice number, the amount and the original due date, re-attach the invoice, and repeat exactly how to pay. Charge a late fee only if it was agreed in your payment terms before the work started. If the invoice stays unpaid after the final notice, pause further work, try a phone call, and only then consider formal escalation.

Late payment is the part of freelancing nobody puts in the brochure. You did the work, you sent the invoice, and then nothing happened. The silence feels personal, but it almost never is. Invoices usually go unpaid for boring, fixable reasons: the email went to the wrong person, the approver was on leave, the invoice is sitting in a queue, or the client simply forgot. Chasing payment is therefore not a confrontation. It is a routine, like sending the invoice itself. This guide gives you that routine: a three-stage reminder sequence with a template for each stage, guidance on late fees and payment terms, and advice on when to pause work and when to escalate. One note first: this post is general information, not legal advice. Late-fee rules and debt-recovery options vary by jurisdiction, so for a large amount, speak to a qualified adviser where you are based.

Before you chase: check the facts first

Before you send any reminder, spend five minutes checking the facts. It saves you from the one mistake that damages a client relationship for no reason: chasing someone who has already paid. Check your bank account and payment app for payments without the invoice reference as well as with it. Check your sent folder: did the invoice go to the right person and address, with the correct amount, due date and payment details? Check whether you sent the right document at all — if the client was expecting a receipt, that confusion alone can hold up payment. Our guide to invoice vs receipt: what's the difference explains which document applies when. Finally, check that you actually agreed a due date. If the invoice says "due on receipt" but the client understood 30 days, you may be chasing a payment that is not late in their mind. If a check turns up a problem on your side, fix it first and say so plainly.

The reminder sequence at a glance

Chasing works best as a sequence decided in advance, not as messages written in frustration. When the timing and tone are planned, each reminder feels calm and professional, and the client always knows what happens next. Use this schedule as your default:

  • Before the due date (large invoices only): a short heads-up two or three days before the due date.
  • Stage 1 — a few days after the due date: a friendly nudge. Assume the invoice was missed, not refused. Re-attach it and ask if anything is needed from you.
  • Stage 2 — about two weeks overdue: a firm reminder. Restate the amount, the due date and how to pay, and ask for a specific payment date.
  • Stage 3 — 30 days overdue: a final notice. State the total now due, including any agreed late fee, and say clearly what happens next.

Send each reminder in the same email thread as the original invoice where you can, so the client sees the full history in one place. Keep every message short, factual and polite, and keep a copy of all of them. That paper trail matters: if the delay ever becomes a dispute, a calm written record is worth far more than a phone call nobody can prove happened. Do not send reminders daily, and do not send the same message twice — one message per stage, on schedule, is enough. If the client replies at any stage, stop the sequence and deal with what they actually said.

Stage 1: the friendly nudge, a few days after the due date

The first reminder resolves most late payments, so it deserves the most care — and the least pressure. Send it three to five days after the due date. Assume a simple mistake: the invoice was missed, the right person never saw it, or the payment is already on its way. Give the client a graceful way to pay without feeling accused. Re-attach the invoice, repeat the amount and the payment details in the body, and ask one question — is there anything you need from me?

Here is a template you can adapt:

Subject: Reminder: Invoice INV-0142 — $850 due 1 December 2026

Hi Sarah, I hope you are well. I wanted to check whether invoice INV-0142 reached you — it was due on 1 December 2026, for $850, and I have not seen the payment yet. I have attached the invoice again for convenience. You can pay by bank transfer using the details on the invoice, with reference INV-0142. If the payment is already on its way, please ignore this note. If there is anything you need from me — a different format, a purchase order number, or approval from someone else — just let me know and I will send it straight away. Best regards, Studio North

It names the invoice, the amount and the due date up front, so the client does not have to open the attachment to understand the request. It offers a face-saving explanation — the payment may already be on its way. And it asks whether something is missing, which is often the real answer: many first reminders come back with "we need a purchase order number" or "please send it to our accounts team", and once you supply that detail, payment follows within days. If you do not know who handles payments, this is the moment to ask. If you want the sending itself to be right, read our guide to how to send an invoice by email.

Stage 2: the firm reminder, about two weeks overdue

If the friendly nudge gets no reply and no payment, wait until about two weeks after the due date and send a firmer reminder. The tone changes now: still polite, but no longer assuming the invoice was simply missed. The client has had two messages and two weeks, so it is fair to ask directly when payment will be made. Your message should also make it easy for the client to tell you if something has changed — a business that has gone quiet may be short of cash, may have lost the person who hired you, or may be disputing the work without telling you. A client embarrassed about cash flow will often pay on a plan if you offer one, but will say nothing if your message gives them no opening.

Here is a template for the second stage:

Subject: Second reminder: Invoice INV-0142 — $850 overdue since 1 December 2026

Hi Sarah, I am following up on invoice INV-0142 for $850, which was due on 1 December 2026 and is now two weeks overdue. I have attached the invoice again, and the payment details are on it — please use reference INV-0142. Could you let me know when I can expect payment, and whether there is anything holding it up? If there is a query about the work or the invoice, I would rather hear it now so we can sort it out. If cash flow is tight at the moment, I am open to discussing a short payment plan. Thank you, Studio North

Three things matter here. First, the subject line says "second reminder" and "overdue", so nobody can mistake this for a first notice. Second, you ask for a specific payment date, not a vague promise — "when can I expect payment" is much harder to dodge than "please pay soon". Third, you name the two commonest real reasons for delay — a query, or cash flow — and invite the client to say so. If the client replies with a date, confirm it in writing and wait until that date passes before sending the next stage.

Stage 3: the final notice, at 30 days overdue

Thirty days overdue is the point where the tone has to change clearly. This is no longer a reminder; it is a final notice, and the client should be able to tell that from the subject line alone. Say what is owed, including any late fee agreed in your terms, say what happens next, and give a short, specific deadline — usually seven days. Stay calm and factual, never angry, and never threaten anything you are not actually prepared to do. Do not write that you will take formal action unless you genuinely intend to, and do not invent a deadline you will not enforce: a final notice followed by nothing teaches the client that your deadlines do not matter.

Here is a template for the final notice:

Subject: Final notice: Invoice INV-0142 — $850 overdue 30 days

Hi Sarah, this is a final notice for invoice INV-0142. The amount of $850 was due on 1 December 2026 and is now 30 days overdue. I have attached the invoice again, and the payment details are on it — please use reference INV-0142. Please arrange payment within seven days, by 15 January 2027. If I have not received payment by then, I will pause all further work on your account, and I will need to consider formal steps to recover the amount owed, as set out in my payment terms. If there is a reason payment has been delayed, please contact me before that date so we can resolve it. Regards, Studio North

Send the final notice by email, and consider a copy by a second route as well — a message through the channel you normally use with the client, or a printed letter if the amount justifies it — so there is no doubt it was received. Keep the language factual: the invoice number, the amount, the original due date, how many days overdue it is, the new deadline, and what happens next. "I will need to consider formal steps" is honest and measured; a specific threat about a particular legal action is something to discuss with an adviser first. Remember that the final notice is also a record — if the matter ever goes further, the person reading it later will judge you by how reasonable you were at this stage.

Late fees: how to set them and charge them fairly

Late fees are where freelancers most often go wrong, usually by adding a fee the client never agreed to. The rule is simple: a late fee is fair only if the client knew about it before the work started. It must be in your payment terms, stated on the invoice, and ideally agreed in writing — in the contract, the proposal, or the email that confirmed the work. A fee invented after the invoice is already overdue will be resented, and a client who resents a fee disputes the whole invoice instead of paying it. Set your late-fee policy once, write it down, and use it the same way for every client.

Your policy should say when the fee starts — for example, after the due date has passed — how it is calculated, and whether it is a one-off amount or charged per period. Keep the wording plain and the fee modest: its purpose is to encourage prompt payment, not to punish the client or to make money from the delay. Apply the fee consistently, and state it on the invoice and in each reminder once it applies, so the client always knows the total now due. Remember that the rules on late fees and interest on overdue payments vary by jurisdiction, and the contract and local rules where you and the client are based decide what you can actually charge. This is general information, not legal advice — for a large or disputed amount, check the position where you are before you add a fee. If you have never set payment terms at all, do it before the next invoice goes out, not in the middle of chasing this one: terms are agreed upfront, not imposed afterwards. Payment terms that prevent the next late payment

Chasing is much easier when the terms were clear from the start, and the best time to fix late payment is before the invoice is sent. Four terms make the biggest difference. First, a clear due date on every invoice — a specific date, not "due on receipt". Second, payment in advance or a deposit for new clients, so the largest amount you ever chase is only part of the fee. Third, payment details on the invoice itself, so the client never has to ask how to pay. Fourth, a short, plain statement of what happens if payment is late — the late fee, if any, and that further work pauses until the account is settled. When those four things are on the invoice, the reminder sequence is simply enforcing what the client already agreed to. For large projects, break the fee into milestones with an invoice at each one, so a delay is caught when only one milestone is unpaid. When to stop work — and how to say it

Pausing work is the most effective step a freelancer has, and the one most freelancers avoid for too long. Every extra hour you work for a client who has not paid is an hour you may never be paid for, and a client who knows you will keep working regardless of payment has no reason to pay promptly. Decide your rule in advance: for most freelancers, no new work starts — and current work pauses — once an invoice is seriously overdue and the reminder sequence has had no reply. Thirty days overdue, with a final notice sent, is a sensible point for most small invoices. For larger amounts, or a client with a history of late payment, pause sooner.

Tell the client in writing, calmly and plainly, before you stop: "As invoice INV-0142 is now 30 days overdue, I will pause work on your project until payment is received. I will resume as soon as the payment arrives, and I am happy to discuss a payment plan if that would help." That states the fact, the consequence, and a way back. It is a business decision, not a punishment, and framing it that way keeps the door open. Do not stop work without telling the client, and do not stop in the middle of something time-critical without warning. Once you have paused, hold the line: resuming work on a promise alone, without payment, simply restarts the same cycle.

The phone call

Some invoices are not resolved by email, and that is when a phone call helps. Email is easy to ignore; a polite voice is harder to put off, and a five-minute call often reveals what three emails could not — the approver is on leave, the invoice needs a purchase order number, or the business is in trouble and needs a plan. Before you call, have the facts in front of you: the invoice number, the amount, the due date, and the dates of your reminders. Keep the call short and factual, ask for a specific payment date, and follow up in writing straight afterwards: "Thank you for speaking just now. As agreed, I will look out for payment of $850 for invoice INV-0142 by 20 December." That email turns a conversation into a record. If the client does not answer, leave one message with the same facts and the same request for a date, and return to the written sequence. If the client says they cannot pay right now, a written payment plan — total, instalments and dates, confirmed by email — is usually better than a standoff, but do not agree to an open-ended plan with no dates, and do not keep working while it is unpaid.

Escalation: collections and small claims as a last resort

If the final notice has passed, the phone call has not worked, and there is still no payment and no plan, you are left with formal escalation — treat it as a last resort, not a next step. The options depend on where you and the client are based, how large the amount is, and what your contract says, so this section is general information only, not legal advice. In broad terms, the formal routes are: a formal letter of demand, stating the amount, the due date and a final deadline; a collections agency, which takes a share of whatever it recovers; and a small-claims or equivalent low-value court process, designed for smaller debts and usually not needing a lawyer. Each costs time, and some cost money, so weigh the amount owed against the cost of recovering it — spending more to chase a small invoice than the invoice is worth is a bad decision, however frustrating the non-payment feels.

Before any formal step, make sure your records are complete: the contract or written agreement, the invoice and every reminder with its date, the client's replies, and proof that the work was delivered and accepted. That file is what any agency, adviser or court will ask for first. Check your contract for anything affecting the route you can take — for example, a clause about where disputes are handled, or a requirement to try mediation first. And get advice before you act: a short consultation with a qualified adviser in your jurisdiction can tell you whether your late fee is enforceable and whether the amount justifies the process at all. For a first late payment from an otherwise good client, a firm final notice and a pause on work will usually resolve it. Reserve formal escalation for the client who has ignored every stage, and be ready to walk away from a small debt if the cost of recovering it is higher than the debt itself.

How to chase an overdue invoice, in short

Check the facts first — has the client paid without the reference, did the invoice go to the right person, and was the amount and due date correct? Then follow the sequence: a friendly nudge a few days after the due date, a firm reminder at about two weeks asking for a specific payment date, and a final notice at 30 days with a seven-day deadline and a clear statement of what happens next. Charge a late fee only if it was agreed in your terms before the work started, and remember that late-fee rules vary by jurisdiction and this is general information, not legal advice. If the sequence brings no payment and no reply, pause further work in writing, try a phone call, and consider a payment plan if the client is genuinely short of cash. Formal escalation — a demand letter, a collections agency, or a small-claims process — is a last resort for the client who has ignored every stage, and it is worth getting advice before you take it. Set the terms right on the next invoice, with a clear due date and a stated late fee, and most of this becomes unnecessary. Frequently asked questions

How long should I wait before chasing an overdue invoice?

Send a friendly reminder a few days after the due date, then a firmer reminder at about two weeks overdue, and a final notice at 30 days. Most late invoices are missed rather than refused, so start by assuming a mistake and giving the client an easy way to pay, and only escalate the tone when the earlier reminders have had no reply.

What should I write in a first reminder for a late invoice?

Keep it short, polite and factual: restate the invoice number, the amount and the original due date, attach the invoice again, and ask whether anything is needed from you. A good first line is 'I wanted to check whether invoice INV-0142 reached you' — it gives the client a graceful way to pay without feeling accused.

Can I charge a late fee on an overdue invoice?

You can charge a late fee only if it was agreed in advance — stated on the invoice and in your payment terms before the work started — and the rules on late fees vary by jurisdiction, so check what applies where you and the client are based. State the fee clearly, apply it consistently, and never add a fee the client did not agree to. This is general information, not legal advice.

When should I stop work for a client who has not paid?

Pause further work when a reminder sequence has had no reply and the invoice is seriously overdue, and tell the client plainly that work will resume once payment is received. Stopping work is a business decision, not a punishment: it protects you from doing more unpaid work, and it gives the client a clear reason to pay. Keep the message calm, factual and in writing.
