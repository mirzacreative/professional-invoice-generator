Invoice Payment Terms Freelancers Should Set

Updated 14 November 2026 · 7-minute read

Quick answer: Every freelance invoice should state when payment is due, how the client can pay, what happens if payment is late, and whether a deposit was agreed. The simplest setup that works for most freelancers is Net 14, a deposit of 30 to 50 percent on new-client projects, one clearly stated late fee, and your preferred payment method listed first. Agree the terms before the work starts, print them on the invoice, and they stop being awkward — they become part of the job.

Payment terms are the part of freelancing most people skip until it costs them money. You do the work, send the invoice, and then wait. A week passes. Two weeks. The client is friendly, the work was approved, and yet the money has not arrived — because the invoice never said when it was due, how to pay, or what happens if it is late. The client is not necessarily avoiding you; without a deadline, your invoice sits behind every invoice that does have one. Clear payment terms fix that. They tell the client exactly what to do and by when, and they give you a polite, professional reason to follow up the day the deadline passes. This guide covers the terms every freelancer should set: Net 7, 14 and 30, due-date wording, late fees, deposits, payment methods, and what to do when a client pays late — with example wording you can copy straight onto your invoice.

Net 7, Net 14, Net 30: which payment term should you choose?

"Net" terms count the days from the invoice date. Net 7 means payment is due within 7 days, Net 14 within 14 days, and Net 30 within 30 days. "Due on receipt" means the client should pay as soon as the invoice arrives. Each choice sends a different message, and the right one depends on the size of the job and the kind of client. For most freelancers, Net 14 is the best default: it is short enough to keep your cash flowing, and long enough for a real person to approve and process the payment. Use due on receipt or Net 7 for small jobs, one-off tasks and clients who pay quickly — there is no reason to wait a month for a few hundred dollars. Use Net 30 only for large companies whose accounts departments genuinely need the time; if a small business asks for Net 30, ask why, and consider Net 14 with a small discount for early payment instead. Whatever term you pick, always print the actual calendar due date next to it — "Net 14 — due 28 November 2026" — so the client never has to calculate anything. Due-date wording: be exact, not polite and vague

Vague due dates are the most common invoicing mistake. "Payment due soon", "please pay at your earliest convenience" and "payment due upon completion" all sound polite and mean nothing — soon is not a date, and earliest convenience is whenever the client feels like it. Exact wording works better and is still professional. Write: "Payment due within 14 days of the invoice date: 28 November 2026." Add the invoice number to the sentence if the client pays by bank transfer, so their finance team can match the payment: "Please quote invoice INV-023 when paying." If the work was delivered in stages, state which stage this invoice covers: "Invoice 2 of 3 — design delivery, balance due on final delivery." The rule is simple: a stranger who has never seen your project should be able to read the invoice and know exactly how much to pay, by when, and how. Late fees: how they work and when to use them

A late fee is a charge added to an overdue invoice, and it only works if it was stated on the invoice before the payment became late. A common wording is a fixed percentage per month on the overdue balance — for example, "A late fee of 1.5% per month applies to balances unpaid after the due date." Keep the rate modest and the wording plain; an aggressive fee scares good clients and is hard to enforce. Before you add a late fee, check the rules where you and your client are based, because some countries cap late payment charges by law, and a fee above the legal limit cannot be enforced. The most important rule about late fees is honesty with yourself: only state a fee you are genuinely willing to charge. A late fee you never enforce teaches the client that your deadlines are flexible, which is worse than having no late fee at all. If you would rather keep the relationship than charge the fee on a first late payment, say so in the reminder — "I am waiving the late fee this once" — so the client knows the fee is real and this was a favour, not a policy.

Deposits and upfront payment: protect yourself before you start

A deposit is money paid before the work begins, and for freelancers it is the single best protection against non-payment. For new clients and larger projects, ask for 30 to 50 percent upfront, with the balance due on delivery. A deposit does two things: it proves the client is serious and able to pay, and it covers your time if the project is cancelled halfway through. For small jobs with trusted repeat clients, invoicing in full on delivery is usually fine — the relationship is the protection. For very large projects, split the payment into milestones instead of one deposit: a third to start, a third at the midpoint, and a third on delivery. State the deposit on the invoice itself — "Deposit received: $450. Balance due: $450" — so the client can see the maths. And never start substantial work for a brand-new client with no deposit and no contract, no matter how enthusiastic they sound. Enthusiasm is not a payment method.

Accepted payment methods: list them, and put your favourite first

Clients pay faster when paying is easy, so list every method you accept and put the one you prefer first. Bank transfer is the standard for larger invoices and costs little or nothing for either side, but it needs your full account details printed correctly on the invoice. Payment services such as PayPal or Stripe are convenient for international clients, but check the fees — a fee of a few percent on every invoice adds up, and you may prefer to build it into your rate rather than pass it on. Payment links are the fastest option for small invoices, because the client can pay in two clicks without leaving their inbox. Whatever you offer, keep the details short and accurate: one wrong digit in an account number can delay payment by weeks. What to do when a client pays late

Most late payments are forgetfulness, not refusal, so start politely and get firmer in stages. On the day after the due date, send a short friendly reminder with the invoice attached again: "Hi, just a quick note that invoice INV-023 for $450 was due yesterday. Could you let me know when it will be paid?" If there is no reply in a week, send a second reminder that mentions the late fee and asks for a specific payment date. If there is still no payment after two to three weeks, call the client — a phone call is harder to ignore than an email, and it often reveals the real reason, such as an approver on holiday or a finance queue. Pause new work for that client until the overdue invoice is settled, and say so plainly: "I will be happy to continue once the outstanding invoice is paid." Keep every message polite and factual, and keep copies of everything. If the amount is large and the client stops responding entirely, a formal demand letter or a small-claims process may be the next step — but most late payments are resolved at the reminder stage, especially when the invoice had a clear due date and a stated late fee.

Example payment terms to copy onto your invoice

Here is a short set of terms you can adapt for your own invoices. Put them near the bottom of the invoice, under the total: "Payment due within 14 days of the invoice date: 28 November 2026. Payment by bank transfer to the account shown above, or via the payment link provided. Please quote invoice INV-023 with your payment. A deposit of 50% was received on 1 November 2026; the balance of $450 is now due. A late fee of 1.5% per month applies to balances unpaid after the due date. Thank you for your business." That is five sentences, and it covers the due date, the method, the reference, the deposit and the late fee. Common payment-term mistakes to avoid

  • No due date at all — an invoice without a due date is paid whenever the client feels like it.
  • Agreeing terms after the work is done — terms belong in the quote or contract, not just on the invoice.
  • Different terms for every client — one standard term, with exceptions you choose deliberately, is easier to manage.
  • A late fee you never enforce — it teaches clients your deadlines are flexible.
  • Starting work for a new client with no deposit — enthusiasm is not a payment method.
  • Missing or wrong payment details — one wrong digit can delay payment by weeks.
  • Chasing late payment angrily — polite, factual reminders get paid faster than angry ones.

Your payment terms, in short

Pick a default term — Net 14 works for most freelancers — and print the calendar due date on every invoice. Ask for a deposit of 30 to 50 percent from new clients and on large projects. State one late fee you are willing to enforce. List your payment methods with your favourite first. Agree all of it before the work starts, and follow up politely the day after the due date. Payment terms are not about being strict; they are about being clear. Clear terms get you paid on time, keep the client relationship professional, and let you spend your time on the work instead of on chasing money. Set them once, put them on every invoice, and let the terms do the chasing for you.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best invoice payment terms for freelancers?

For most freelancers, Net 14 is a good default: short enough to keep cash flowing, long enough for a client to process the payment. Use due on receipt for small jobs under a few hundred dollars, and Net 30 only for large companies whose accounts departments genuinely need the time. Whatever you choose, agree it in writing before the work starts and print it on every invoice.

What does Net 30 mean on an invoice?

Net 30 means the client must pay within 30 days of the invoice date. Net 14 means 14 days, Net 7 means 7 days, and due on receipt means payment is expected as soon as the invoice arrives. Always print the actual calendar due date next to the term, so the client never has to work it out.

Can freelancers charge a late fee?

Yes, if the late fee was stated on the invoice and agreed with the client before the work began. A common wording is a fixed percentage per month on overdue balances, such as 1.5% per month. Check the rules in your country and your client's country first, because some places cap late fees by law — and only state a fee you are genuinely willing to charge.

Should freelancers ask for a deposit before starting work?

For new clients and larger projects, yes. A deposit of 30 to 50 percent before work starts protects you if the project is cancelled and shows the client is serious. For small jobs with trusted repeat clients, invoicing in full on delivery is usually fine. Put the deposit amount, the payment date and the balance due on the invoice itself.
