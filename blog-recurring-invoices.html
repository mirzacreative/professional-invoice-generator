Recurring Invoices: How to Bill Repeat Clients (2026)

Updated 20 November 2026 · 8-minute read

Quick answer: A recurring invoice is the same invoice, sent automatically on a schedule — monthly is the most common cycle — for the same service at the same price. Set it up once, with the client, amount, cycle and due date agreed in writing, and each invoice in the series goes out with its own unique number and its own due date. If the scope or the price changes, tell the client in writing before the next invoice, and update the template — never change the amount on an invoice that has already been sent. A recurring invoice does not collect payment by itself: pair it with a clear payment method and a follow-up habit for the months a client pays late.

The first time a client becomes a repeat client, the invoicing changes. One-off work is simple: finish the job, send the invoice, get paid, move on. Repeat work is different. The same work happens every month, the same amount is due every month, and if you create each invoice by hand, you will forget one eventually — usually the month you are busiest, which is the month you most need the money. A recurring invoice solves that by turning a monthly chore into a system: you set it up once, and the invoice goes out on schedule whether you remember it or not. This guide explains what a recurring invoice is, when it makes sense and when it does not, how to set one up, how to choose the cycle, how to handle changes, pauses and cancellations, and what to do when a client does not pay. One note first: this post is general information, not legal or tax advice. The rules on invoicing vary by jurisdiction, so for a specific question about what applies where you are based, check with a qualified adviser.

What a recurring invoice is — and what it is not

A recurring invoice is an invoice that is sent automatically on a regular schedule for the same service at the same price, without you creating it from scratch each time. It is used for work that repeats: a monthly retainer, a website maintenance plan, a social media package, a hosting or support plan, a subscription, or an ongoing service contract. Each invoice in the series is a separate invoice with its own number and its own due date, even though the amount and the description stay the same. That last point matters: a recurring invoice is not one invoice sent again and again. It is a new invoice, every cycle, with a new number, for the same recurring service.

It is also not the same thing as an automatic payment. A recurring invoice sends the bill; it does not take the money. Some tools combine the two — an invoice that is sent and charged automatically — but the invoice and the payment are separate steps, and you should be clear with the client about which one you have set up. A client who agreed to receive a monthly invoice has not necessarily agreed to an automatic charge on their card. If you want automatic payment, agree it separately and in writing, and keep the invoice as the record of what was charged and why.

When recurring invoices make sense — and when they do not

Recurring invoices make sense when the work and the price are stable. A monthly retainer for a fixed number of hours, a maintenance plan at a fixed monthly fee, a content package with a fixed number of posts — if the scope is the same every cycle and the price is the same every cycle, a recurring invoice is the right tool. It also makes sense when the client wants predictability: a client who budgets monthly would rather see the same invoice on the same day each month than a different invoice at a different time.

They do not make sense when the work changes every cycle. If the hours vary, the deliverables vary, and the price varies, a recurring invoice will be wrong most months — and an invoice that has to be corrected every month is worse than no system at all. In that case, send a normal invoice each cycle, with the actual hours or deliverables on it, and keep the recurring system for the clients whose work is genuinely fixed. They also do not make sense for a client with a short engagement: if the work lasts two months, setting up a recurring schedule is more effort than sending two invoices by hand. Use the recurring system for the clients you expect to bill for many months, and keep the simple approach for the rest.

Setting up a recurring invoice

Setting one up takes a few minutes, but it should start with a conversation, not with a button. Agree the following with the client, in writing, before the first invoice goes out: what the service is, exactly; how much it costs per cycle; how often the invoice is sent; on what day of the cycle; when payment is due; and how either side can change or end the arrangement. Put those terms in the contract or in a short email the client confirms. The invoice system can only repeat what you told it to repeat, so the agreement has to be clear before the automation starts.

Then set up the template. Give it a clear description of the service — "Monthly website maintenance — November 2026", for example, with the month changing each cycle — and the agreed amount. Set the cycle to match the agreement: monthly is the most common, but weekly, quarterly and yearly are all valid. Set the send date: the first of the month, the anniversary of the start date, or the date the client asked for. Set the due date using the payment terms you have agreed — for example, payment due 14 days after the invoice date. And set the numbering: each invoice in the series must have its own unique, sequential number. Choosing the cycle: monthly, quarterly, or something else

Monthly is the default for a reason. Most clients budget monthly, most retainers are priced monthly, and a monthly invoice is small enough that a late payment is a small problem. It is the right cycle for retainers, maintenance plans, and ongoing service packages. Weekly works for short, intensive engagements — a few weeks of full-time work where the client wants to pay as the work progresses. Quarterly works when the amounts are small and the client prefers fewer, larger payments, or when the service is delivered in quarterly blocks. Yearly works for annual plans: a yearly support plan, an annual licence, a yearly maintenance contract where the client pays once and the service runs for twelve months.

Match the cycle to the work and to the client's cash flow, not to your convenience. A client who pays monthly from a monthly budget will struggle with a quarterly invoice that is three times the size. A client who wants one annual payment will be annoyed by twelve small invoices. Ask the client which cycle they prefer, write it into the agreement, and keep it the same. If you change the cycle, tell the client in writing first and explain why — a client who expected a monthly invoice and receives a quarterly one for three times the amount will assume it is a mistake, and they may not pay it until you explain.

Changes, pauses, and cancellations

Recurring work changes, and the invoice system has to change with it — but the change must always happen before the next invoice, never on an invoice that has already been sent. A price change: tell the client in writing, explain what is changing and why, give the date it takes effect, and update the template so the new amount applies from that date. A rate increase that appears on an invoice without warning is a dispute waiting to happen. A scope change: if the client adds work or removes work, update the description and the amount, and confirm the change in writing. If the change is temporary — an extra month of work, or a discount for one month — add a separate line to that month's invoice and note the reason, rather than changing the recurring amount itself. That keeps the template clean and the change visible.

A pause: a client may ask to pause for a month — a holiday, a quiet period, a budget freeze. Agree the pause in writing, stop the invoices for that period, and agree the restart date. Do not keep sending invoices during a pause and do not delete the template: a paused template is easy to restart, and a deleted one has to be rebuilt from scratch. A cancellation: agree the end date in writing, send a final invoice for any work up to that date, and then stop the series. If the client cancels mid-cycle, check the agreement: some contracts require payment for the full cycle, others for the work done up to the cancellation date. Whatever the agreement says, follow it — and send the final invoice promptly, while the work is still fresh in the client's mind. If the final invoice is a different amount from the usual one, explain the difference on the invoice itself, in one line.

Getting paid on time, every cycle

A recurring invoice does not guarantee payment. It guarantees the bill goes out; the money still has to arrive. Three habits make the difference. First, make payment easy: put the payment details on every invoice, in the same place, and offer the method the client actually uses — a bank transfer, a card link, or whatever you agreed. A client who has to ask how to pay will pay late. Second, send the invoice on the same day every cycle. A client who receives the invoice on the first of the month, every month, builds it into their routine; a client who receives it on a different day each month has to think about it each time, and thinking takes time.

Third, follow up on the same schedule every cycle. Check which invoices are unpaid a few days after the due date, and send a polite reminder. Do not wait a month and then send three reminders at once — that is how a small late payment becomes a large one. If a client pays late every month, talk to them about it: a different send date, a different due date, or a different payment method may be all it takes. Record-keeping for a recurring series

A recurring series creates a lot of invoices, and the record behind them matters more, not less. Keep a simple list — a spreadsheet is enough — with one row per invoice: the number, the date, the client, the amount, the due date, and the status (sent, paid, overdue, void). Update it every time an invoice goes out, not at the end of the quarter. The list is what lets you check, at a glance, that the series has no gaps and no duplicates, and it is what your accountant will ask for first. It is also how you spot a client who has slipped into a pattern of late payment before it becomes a problem.

Keep the invoices themselves, too: a copy of every invoice in the series, in order, in one folder, named by number. Keep cancelled or voided invoices in the same folder, marked as void, not deleted. And keep the agreement behind the series — the contract or the email that set the amount, the cycle and the terms — with the invoices, so that anyone reading the record can see why the invoices exist and what they were for. A recurring series with no records behind it is a system nobody can check, and a system nobody can check is a system that will fail the first time someone asks about it. The same numbering rules apply to a recurring series as to any other invoice: one sequence, every client, no duplicates, no renumbering — and if you are unsure whether an invoice is different enough to be a separate document, read our guide on the difference between an invoice and a receipt before you send it.

Recurring invoices, in short

Use a recurring invoice when the work and the price are stable: agree the service, the amount, the cycle and the due date with the client in writing, then set up a template that sends a new invoice each cycle with its own unique number. Monthly is the most common cycle, but match it to the client's budget and the work. Change the amount or the scope only with written notice before the next invoice, pause or cancel in writing, and keep a simple list of every invoice in the series with its number, date, amount and status. Remember that a recurring invoice sends the bill — it does not take the money — so make payment easy, send on the same day every cycle, and follow up promptly when a payment is late. Frequently asked questions

What is a recurring invoice?

A recurring invoice is an invoice that is sent automatically on a regular schedule — weekly, monthly, quarterly or yearly — for the same service at the same price, without you having to create it from scratch each time. It is used for work that repeats: a monthly retainer, a hosting or maintenance plan, a subscription, or an ongoing service contract. Each invoice in the series is a separate invoice with its own number and due date, even though the amount and the description stay the same.

How often should I send a recurring invoice?

Match the invoice cycle to the work and to how the client pays. Monthly is the most common cycle for retainers and ongoing services, because it matches how most clients budget. Weekly works for short, intensive engagements, and quarterly or yearly works for annual plans where the client prefers fewer, larger payments. Pick the cycle that matches the agreement you made with the client, write it into the contract, and keep it the same — changing the cycle without telling the client is a fast way to create a payment dispute.

Can I change the amount on a recurring invoice?

Yes, but not silently. If the scope changes, the price changes, or a rate increase takes effect, tell the client in writing before the next invoice goes out, explain what is changing and why, and update the recurring template so the new amount applies from the agreed date. Do not change the amount on an invoice that has already been sent. If the change is temporary — an extra month of work, or a discount for one month — add a separate line to that month's invoice and note the reason, rather than changing the recurring amount itself.

What happens if a client does not pay a recurring invoice?

Treat a missed recurring invoice like any other overdue invoice: follow up promptly, in writing, with the invoice number, the amount and the due date. Check whether the service should continue while the invoice is unpaid — your contract should say whether work pauses after a missed payment, and for how long. Do not keep sending new invoices for work you have not been paid for without a word: a short, polite reminder after a few days, then a firmer one, and then a decision about whether to pause the service, is the usual pattern.
