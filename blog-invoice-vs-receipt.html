Invoice vs Receipt: What's the Difference?

Updated 16 November 2026 · 7-minute read

Quick answer: An invoice is a request for payment — you send it before the money arrives, and it says what is owed, by when, and how to pay. A receipt is proof of payment — you send it after the money arrives, and it confirms the amount paid, the date, and the payment method. An invoice asks for money; a receipt proves it was received. Your business needs both: the invoice to get paid, and the receipt to close the transaction and keep clean records. Mixing them up is one of the most common bookkeeping mistakes freelancers and small businesses make, and it causes real problems — for your clients' accounts, for your tax records, and for any payment dispute.

The confusion is understandable. Both documents list the same business, the same client, the same items and the same amounts, and both look like a piece of paper with a total on it. But they do opposite jobs, at opposite ends of a transaction, and using one where the other belongs creates trouble. Send a receipt before payment and the client thinks they have already paid. Send an invoice after payment and the client thinks you are asking twice. Send neither and you have no record at all. This guide explains the difference clearly, shows what each document must include, when to send each one, and the mistakes that cause the most confusion — so your paperwork is right every time.

What is an invoice?

An invoice is a formal request for payment, sent after you have delivered work or goods but before you have been paid. It tells the client exactly what they owe, what it is for, when it is due, and how to pay. A proper invoice includes your business name and contact details, the client's name and address, a unique invoice number, the invoice date, a description of each item or service, the quantity and price of each, any tax, the total amount due, the payment due date, and your payment details — bank account, payment link, or other method. It may also include payment terms, such as a late fee or a deposit already received. The invoice is a legal record of a debt: it creates an obligation for the client to pay, and it gives you the basis for following up if they do not. For freelancers, the invoice is also the document that starts the payment clock — the due date on the invoice is the date the client is expected to pay by, and the day after it passes is the day you can politely chase. What is a receipt?

A receipt is proof that payment has been received, sent after the money arrives. It confirms that the transaction is complete. A proper receipt includes your business name and contact details, the client's name, a unique receipt number, the date the payment was received, a description of what was paid for, the amount received, the payment method used — bank transfer, card, cash, or a payment service — and any remaining balance if the payment was partial. It does not include a due date, because nothing is due anymore; the payment has happened. The receipt matters for both sides: the client needs it as proof of purchase for their own accounts and tax records, and you need it as proof that the money was received and the invoice is settled. For cash payments especially, a receipt is essential — without one, there is no record the payment ever happened, and that is a problem for both of you if the payment is ever questioned. Many businesses send the receipt by email the same day the payment arrives, so the transaction is closed while it is still fresh.

When to send each one

The order is always the same: invoice first, receipt second. Send the invoice when the work is delivered or the goods are shipped, with the due date clearly stated. Do not send the invoice before the work is done unless a deposit was agreed — an invoice for work not yet delivered confuses the client and weakens your position if there is a dispute. Once the payment arrives, send the receipt promptly, ideally the same day. A quick receipt tells the client the payment was received and the matter is closed, which builds trust — a client who pays and hears nothing wonders whether the money arrived. For recurring work, the rhythm is monthly: one invoice per month, one receipt per payment. For a large project paid in stages, send one invoice per milestone and one receipt per payment received, so each stage has its own clean paper trail. If a client pays in advance, the order reverses slightly: take the deposit, send a receipt for the deposit, and then send the invoice for the balance when the work is delivered, showing the deposit already received. Whatever the pattern, never send a receipt for money you have not received, and never send an invoice for money already paid without marking it clearly as paid.

Can one document be both an invoice and a receipt?

Sometimes, but only in one specific case: when a client pays immediately at the point of sale. A shop till receipt that shows the items, the total, the payment method and "paid" is effectively both — it records the sale and proves the payment, because both happened at the same moment. For freelancers and service businesses, the situation is different, because payment almost never happens at the same moment as delivery. The practical answer is to keep them separate. Once an invoice is paid, you can mark the original invoice as "Paid" and send a copy to the client as confirmation — that is common and acceptable — but the cleanest approach is a separate receipt with its own number, its own date, and the payment method stated. Separate documents are also better for your records: your invoice numbers track what you billed, your receipt numbers track what you received, and the two lists should match at the end of the month. If they do not match, you can see immediately which invoices are still unpaid — and that is the information your business runs on. What each document must include

Both documents share the basics: your business name and contact details, the client's name, a unique number, a date, a description of the items or services, and the amounts. The differences are what matter. The invoice must include a payment due date and payment terms, because it is a request for payment — without a due date, the client has no deadline, and without terms, you have no basis for a late fee or a follow-up. The receipt must include the payment date and the payment method, because it is proof of payment — without those, it does not prove anything. The invoice should also show your payment details clearly, so the client can pay without having to ask. The receipt should show the remaining balance if the payment was partial — "Received $450 of $900; balance $450 due" — so there is no confusion about what is left. Keep the numbering separate and sequential: invoice numbers in one series, receipt numbers in another, so you can find either document in seconds. And keep copies of everything, in the same place, for at least the period your local tax rules require — usually several years.

Tax and record-keeping: why the distinction matters

For tax purposes, invoices and receipts play different roles, and mixing them up can cost you money. Invoices record what you billed — your income for the period, even if some of it has not arrived yet. Receipts record what you actually received — the cash that came in. Many tax systems treat these differently, and your accountant will need both lists to prepare your return correctly. For your clients, the distinction matters just as much: a business client cannot claim an expense from an unpaid invoice, because they have not spent the money yet — they need the receipt as proof of payment. If you send a client an invoice and they file it as an expense before paying, their books will be wrong, and they may come back to you to fix it. The rule is simple: the invoice is your record of the sale, the receipt is your client's record of the purchase, and both are your records of the business. Keep them separate, number them separately, and store them together by month, so any transaction can be traced from invoice to receipt in a minute.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Sending a receipt before payment — the client thinks they have already paid, and chasing becomes awkward.
  • Sending an invoice after payment — the client thinks you are asking twice, and trust takes a knock.
  • Using an unpaid invoice as proof of purchase — it proves a debt, not a payment, and it will not satisfy an accountant.
  • No receipt for cash payments — without one, there is no record the payment ever happened.
  • Same number series for both documents — it makes finding a specific invoice or receipt slow and error-prone.
  • No due date on the invoice — an invoice without a deadline is paid whenever the client feels like it.
  • No payment date or method on the receipt — a receipt without those details does not prove anything.
  • Forgetting to send the receipt at all — the client wonders whether the payment arrived, and your records stay open.

Invoice vs receipt, in short

An invoice asks for money; a receipt proves it arrived. Send the invoice when the work is done, with a clear due date and payment details. Send the receipt the day the payment arrives, with the payment date, the method, and the amount received. Number them separately, keep copies of both, and never send one in place of the other. It is a small discipline, but it is the difference between clean books and a mess at tax time — and between a client who trusts your paperwork and one who does not. Frequently asked questions

Is an invoice the same as a receipt?

No. An invoice is a request for payment sent before the money arrives — it says what is owed, by when, and how to pay. A receipt is proof of payment sent after the money arrives — it confirms the amount paid, the date, and the payment method. An invoice asks for money; a receipt proves it was received.

Can I use an invoice as a receipt?

Not properly. An unpaid invoice does not prove payment, so a client cannot use it as proof of purchase for their records or tax return. Once an invoice is paid, you can mark it as paid and send it as confirmation, but the cleanest approach is to issue a separate receipt that states the payment date, the amount received and the method used.

Do I need to send a receipt for every payment?

It is good practice to send one for every payment, even if the client does not ask. A receipt closes the transaction, gives the client a record for their accounts, and protects you if a payment is ever disputed. For small cash sales, a simple written or emailed receipt with the date, amount and your business details is enough.

What must an invoice include that a receipt does not?

An invoice must include a payment due date and payment terms — when the money is due and what happens if it is late — because it is a request for payment. A receipt does not need a due date, but it must include the payment date and the method used, because it is proof that the payment happened. Both must show the seller, the buyer, the items, and the amounts.
