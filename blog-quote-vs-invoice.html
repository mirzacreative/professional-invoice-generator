Quote vs Invoice: When to Send Each (and What to Include)

Updated 24 November 2026 · 10-minute read

Quick answer: A quote is sent before the work, to tell the client what you will do and what it will cost, so the client can accept or decline. An invoice is sent after the work, or on an agreed date, to tell the client what was done, what is owed, and when and how to pay. The two documents look alike — both carry your details, the client's details, a description, and a total — and that similarity is exactly why they get mixed up. The difference is timing and purpose, and getting it wrong causes real problems: a client who receives an "invoice" before any work has been agreed may file it as a bill and ignore it, a client who receives a "quote" after the work is done may treat it as an estimate and delay payment, and a quote that is worded as a promise can be held against you when the scope changes. This guide explains the difference in plain language, shows what each document must contain, works through when to send each one, and explains how an accepted quote becomes an invoice, with a worked example. It is general information, not legal or tax advice: document rules and tax requirements vary by jurisdiction, so check the rules that apply where you are based, and ask a qualified adviser for a specific question.

Freelancers meet the quote-versus-invoice question at the very start of a job, usually in a message from a new client: "Can you send me a quote?" or "Just invoice me when you are done." Both requests sound simple, and both are easy to answer badly. Sending an invoice when the client asked for a quote pressures the client to pay for work that has not been agreed. Sending a quote when the work is already done, and the client is waiting to pay, delays payment for no reason. Sending a document with the wrong title — a quote headed "Invoice", or an invoice headed "Quote" — creates confusion in the client's finance team, because the title is how a document is filed, approved, and paid. The habit to build is a simple one: before the work, quote; after the work, invoice; and keep the two documents separate, each with its own number, its own date, and its own purpose, linked by a reference that shows they belong to the same job. The rest of this guide turns that habit into a working process, field by field, with the related guides on this site linked where they cover a topic in depth. Our step-by-step guide on how to write an invoice covers the invoice itself in detail; this guide covers the document that comes before it, and the line between the two.

What a Quote Is

A quote, also called a quotation or a price estimate, is a written offer. It says: here is what I will do, here is what it will cost, here is how long the price holds, and here is what the price includes and excludes. The client reads it, and then accepts it, asks for a change, negotiates, or declines it. Nothing is owed until the client accepts, and no work should start on the strength of a quote alone unless the client has accepted it in writing. A quote is a sales document and a planning document at the same time. For the client, it is the basis for a decision: can I afford this, does it cover what I need, and do I trust this supplier? For you, it is the basis for the job: once the client accepts, the quote becomes the description of what you have agreed to deliver, at what price, and on what terms, and the invoice that follows should match it. That is why a quote must be written with the same care as an invoice, even though no money is being asked for yet. A vague quote — "design work, about £500" — invites a disagreement later about what "design work" meant, and about whether £500 was a fixed price, an estimate, or a starting point. A clear quote — with the deliverables listed, the price stated, the validity period given, and the exclusions named — prevents that disagreement, because both sides can point to the same document.

A quote is not a bill, and it must never look like one. Do not title it "Invoice", do not give it an invoice number, and do not state a payment due date as if money were already owed. Title it "Quote" or "Quotation", give it a quote reference, and date it with the date it was issued. If the client pays a deposit before the work starts, that deposit is usually billed on an invoice, or on a proforma invoice, not on the quote itself — see our guide to proforma invoices for when a proforma document is the right one. A quote may state that a deposit is required before work starts, and how much it will be, but the quote itself is not the request for that deposit. Keep the distinction clean: the quote proposes, the client accepts, and then a separate document asks for money. Clients, accountants, and tax authorities all rely on that distinction, and blurring it is how a quote ends up filed as an unpaid bill, or an invoice ends up filed as an unaccepted estimate.

What an Invoice Is

An invoice is a request for payment for work supplied, goods delivered, or a service performed, or for an amount due on an agreed date. It says: here is what was done, here is what is owed, here is when payment is due, and here is how to pay. It is a business and accounting document, not a sales document: the client's finance team will file it, match it against the quote or the agreement, approve it, and pay it, and your accountant will rely on it as the record of the sale. An invoice carries a unique invoice number, an issue date, and — in most jurisdictions — a set of required details that a quote does not need, including tax details where they apply. Our guide on how to write an invoice walks through every field in order, with a worked example, and our guide to invoice numbering explains the numbering rules: one sequence, no gaps, no reuse, and no renumbering after the invoice has been sent. This guide does not repeat that detail. It covers the invoice only where the invoice differs from the quote, so that you can keep the two documents straight.

An invoice is also the document that starts the payment clock. The due date on the invoice, read with the payment terms, tells the client when to pay, and a late payment is measured from that date. A quote has no due date, because nothing is due. That is the practical difference clients feel most: a quote can sit on a desk while a decision is made, and nobody is late; an invoice that sits on a desk is a payment that is getting later. For that reason, an invoice should never be sent before the work it bills for has been done, or before the date the agreement says it will be sent, unless the agreement provides for an advance or a deposit. Sending an invoice early, in the hope of being paid early, usually backfires: the client queries it, the finance team returns it, and the payment process starts later than it would have if the invoice had been sent at the right time. The invoice's job is to be clear, complete, and on time. The quote's job, done earlier, is to make the invoice uncontroversial when it arrives.

Quote vs Invoice at a Glance

The table below is not a substitute for the explanations that follow, but it is a useful summary to keep beside you when you are deciding which document to send. The two documents share many fields — your details, the client's details, a description, and a total — and the differences are in the fields that only one of them carries, and in the status each one has.

Quote: sent before the work; titled "Quote" or "Quotation"; carries a quote reference and an issue date; states the price offered, the scope, and a validity period; states what is included and excluded; may state payment terms that will apply if accepted; asks for acceptance, not payment; becomes the basis for the agreement once accepted; is not a demand for money and should not be filed as a bill. Invoice: sent after the work, or on an agreed date; titled "Invoice"; carries a unique invoice number and an issue date; states what was actually done, the amount owed, any tax, and the total due; states the payment terms, the due date, and how to pay; may refer to the quote reference and to any purchase order number; asks for payment; is the record of the sale for accounting and tax purposes. The shared fields must match between the two documents: the same business name, the same client name and address, the same description of the work, and — unless the scope changed by agreement — the same price. A client who compares the quote and the invoice and finds a different price, a different description, or a different client name will query the invoice, and the query will delay payment. Matching the documents is the simplest form of quality control in freelance billing.

Two related documents are often confused with quotes and invoices, and both are covered in their own guides on this site. A proforma invoice looks like an invoice but is not a demand for payment: it is used, for example, to show the value of goods for customs, or to ask for payment in advance in some trades. Our proforma invoice guide explains when a document should be labelled as proforma instead of as an invoice. A receipt is issued after payment, to confirm that money was received: it looks back at a payment that has already happened, where an invoice looks forward to a payment that is owed. Our guide on the difference between an invoice and a receipt explains that distinction, including when a receipt is needed and what it should show. If you are unsure which of the four documents — quote, invoice, proforma invoice, or receipt — a situation calls for, ask which point in the job you are at: before the work, a quote; after the work, an invoice; after the payment, a receipt; and for customs or an advance in the circumstances the proforma guide describes, a proforma invoice. The point in the job decides the document.

When to Send a Quote

Send a quote whenever the client needs to decide whether to go ahead, and whenever the price, the scope, or the terms could be misunderstood. In practice, that means: a new client, a new type of work, a job above a small value, a job whose scope is open to interpretation, a job that needs approval from someone other than the person you are dealing with, or a job with a deadline, a deposit, or special terms. A quote is also the right document when the client asks for one, which happens often in business-to-business work: the client needs a written price to get approval, to compare suppliers, or to raise a purchase order. In that case, the quote is not optional. It is the document the client's approval process runs on, and a quote that is slow, vague, or hard to read can lose the job to a competitor whose quote was clear and prompt. Send the quote quickly — ideally within a day or two of the enquiry, and sooner for a simple job — because a quote that arrives a week late arrives after the decision has often been made.

A quote is not needed for every transaction. Small, routine, or repeat jobs, where the price and the scope are already agreed and the client simply wants the work done, are often agreed by a short email or message, with the invoice following the work. A regular client who orders the same service every month does not need a fresh quote each month, although a periodic written confirmation of the price and terms is good practice, and a recurring invoice arrangement may suit the work. The test is whether the client, or anyone else who will see the document, could reasonably misunderstand the price or the scope. If the answer is yes, write a quote. If the answer is no, a short written confirmation is enough — but it must still be in writing. Verbal agreements, remembered differently by each side, are the most common source of freelance payment disputes. Whatever the size of the job, confirm the price, the scope, and the payment terms in writing before the work starts. A quote is the formal way to do that. An email confirmation is the informal way. Silence is not a way at all.

When to Send an Invoice

Send an invoice as soon as the work is complete, or on the date the agreement says — for example, on completion, weekly, or monthly for ongoing work — and never before the work it bills for, unless the agreement provides for a deposit or an advance. Invoicing promptly matters, because the client's payment process starts when the invoice arrives, and a delay of a week in sending is usually a delay of a week in being paid. Agree the timing in writing before the work starts, as part of the quote or the confirmation, so that the invoice date is never a surprise: "Invoice on completion, payment within 14 days" is a complete invoicing arrangement, stated in one line. If the agreement includes a deposit, the deposit is usually billed before work starts, on an invoice or a proforma invoice as the circumstances require, and the balance is billed on completion, with the deposit credited on the face of the final invoice. Our guide to invoice payment terms explains the common terms and how to word them, and our guide to how to write an invoice covers the sending step, including the worked example of a complete invoice.

Do not send an invoice for work that has not been accepted, or for a price the client has not agreed to, in the hope that the client will pay it anyway. An invoice that does not match the quote, or that bills for extra work the client never agreed to, will be queried, and the query will cost more time than the quote that would have prevented it. If the scope changed during the job, agree the change and the price before you invoice for it, as explained in the section on changes below, and show the change on the invoice so the client can see it. If the client asked for a quote and has not yet accepted it, do not send an invoice. Follow up on the quote instead, politely, and ask whether the client would like to go ahead. An invoice sent to a client who has not accepted the job is not a payment request. It is a misunderstanding, and it damages the relationship. The order is fixed: quote, acceptance, work, invoice. Keep it in that order, and the billing process will run smoothly.

What a Quote Must Contain

A good quote contains everything the client needs to make a decision, and everything you need to deliver the job without a further argument. Work through the list below, in order, and check each item before you send the quote. Title. "Quote" or "Quotation", prominent at the top. Quote reference. A unique reference for the quote, in its own sequence, separate from the invoice sequence. A simple sequence — Q-001, Q-002 — or a dated reference — Q-2026-014 — both work, provided every quote has a unique reference and the sequence is kept. Issue date. The date the quote was issued. Validity period. How long the price holds: "This quote is valid for 30 days from the date above", or a specific expiry date. A quote without a validity period can be accepted months later, when your costs have changed, and the argument about whether the price still stands is one you will lose. Your details. Business name, address, email, and phone number, as on your invoices. Client's details. The name and address to be quoted, and the contact person's name and email. Use the exact name the client gave you. Description of the work. Line by line, as specific as on an invoice: what will be delivered, in what form, by when, and what is included. "Logo design: three concepts, two revision rounds, final files in PNG and SVG" is a description. "Design work" is not. Price. Line by line, with quantities and unit prices where they apply, a subtotal, any discount, any tax shown clearly with the basis, and a total. State the currency. State whether the price is fixed or an estimate, and if it is an estimate, state what could change it and by how much. Exclusions. What the price does not include: stock photography, printing, travel, third-party costs, extra revision rounds, or anything else the client might assume is included. Exclusions prevent the most common quote dispute, which is the client assuming something was included and you assuming it was not. Payment terms that will apply. The deposit, if any, the payment terms, and the due date that will appear on the invoice, so the client agrees the terms before the work starts, not after it. Acceptance instruction. How the client accepts: reply to the email, sign and return the quote, or confirm in writing. A quote that does not say how to accept it leaves the acceptance ambiguous, and ambiguity is where disputes start.

Two habits make quotes better. The first is to state assumptions. If the price assumes the client will supply text, images, or access by a certain date, say so. If the price assumes one round of feedback within a week, say so. Assumptions that are written down can be discussed before the job starts. Assumptions that are unwritten become arguments during the job. The second habit is to keep the quote to one page or two, even for a substantial job, with the detail in an attached scope or in the description lines. A quote that runs to ten pages will not be read, and a client who has not read the quote has not really accepted it. Put the price, the scope, the validity, and the terms on the first page, and the detail behind them. If the job is complex, a short covering message — "The quote is attached; the price covers the three items listed; the validity is 30 days; please let me know if you would like any change" — helps the client read it in the right order. And keep a copy of every quote you send, with the date and the reference, in the same folder as the job. A quote you cannot find is a quote you cannot rely on when the invoice is queried.

What an Invoice Must Contain

An invoice contains the same identifying details as a quote — your details, the client's details, a description, and a total — plus the fields that make it a demand for payment and a record of a sale. Our how-to-write-an-invoice guide covers each field in full, so this section is a summary, focused on the fields that differ from a quote. Title. "Invoice", prominent at the top. Invoice number. Unique, sequential, in one sequence for all clients, with no gaps and no reuse, as explained in our invoice numbering guide. Issue date. The date the invoice is issued, not the date the work was done. Description of the work done. Line by line, matching the quote, with quantities, unit prices, and line totals. If the scope changed by agreement, show the change, so the invoice explains itself. Subtotal, tax, and total due. Each on its own line, labelled clearly, in one currency, with the tax rate and basis shown where tax applies. Payment terms and due date. When payment is due, stated as a date, a period, or both, as explained in our payment terms guide. How to pay. The bank details, payment link, or method the client should use, checked carefully, with the invoice number as the reference to quote. References. The quote reference the invoice follows, and any purchase order number, cost code, or department the client supplied, so the finance team can match the invoice to the approval. Tax and legal details. Any tax number, registration number, or other detail your jurisdiction requires on an invoice. A quote does not need these details, because it is not a tax document. An invoice may need them, and leaving them off can cause the invoice to be returned.

The invoice should also credit any deposit or advance already received, on the face of the invoice, as a separate line: total, less advance received, balance due. The client must never be charged the full amount again after paying a deposit, and the credit must be in plain figures, not in a footnote. A client who can see, at a glance, that the invoice follows the quote will approve it without a query. A client who cannot see the link will ask for it, and the payment will wait.

How a Quote Becomes an Invoice: The Workflow

The workflow from quote to invoice is a short, fixed sequence, and it is worth following it the same way every time. Step 1: Send the quote. Send it by email, with the quote attached as a PDF, and the key details in the body of the email: the reference, the total, the validity, and a line asking the client to confirm acceptance. Keep the email short. The quote does the explaining. Step 2: Get acceptance in writing. Wait for a written acceptance — a reply to the email, a signed quote, or a purchase order — before you start the work. A verbal "go ahead" is better than nothing, but it is not a record, and it will not help if the scope is later disputed. If the client accepts with a change, treat the changed quote as a new quote, and get acceptance of the new version. Step 3: Do the work, and record it. Keep a record of what was done, when, and for how long, as you go. If the scope changes, stop and agree the change in writing before you do the extra work, as explained below. Step 4: Issue the invoice. Issue it on completion, or on the agreed date, using the next invoice number, the issue date, the description from the quote, and the price from the quote, with any agreed changes shown. Refer to the quote reference on the invoice. Step 5: Send the invoice and file the set. Send it by the method the client prefers, as explained in our guide to how to send an invoice by email, and file the quote, the acceptance, the invoice, and the payment record together, in one folder per client per job. That folder is the audit trail for the sale, and it is what you will want if the invoice is ever questioned.

Use it as a model: replace every detail with your own, keep the structure, and check each field before you send either document.

Quote — Q-2026-031, issued 10 November 2026, valid for 30 days. From: Maya Ahmed, Freelance Graphic Designer, 14 Rose Lane, Springfield — maya.ahmed@example.com. To: Greenfield Bakery Ltd, Attn: Accounts, 8 Market Street, Springfield. Description: Brand refresh — logo redesign, colour palette, and social media templates. Acceptance: Greenfield Bakery replies on 12 November 2026, accepting the quote and confirming the purchase order number PO-2026-118. Invoice — INV-014, issued 23 November 2026. Description as on the quote, with the addition of one agreed extra: stock photography licence, 35.00 GBP, agreed by email on 18 November 2026. Subtotal: 635.00 GBP. Balance due: 462.00 GBP, due within 14 days, by 7 December 2026, quoting INV-014. The invoice matches the quote, shows the agreed extra and the deposit credit, and can be approved without a query. That is the standard to aim for, on every job.

Validity, Changes, and When a Quote Becomes Binding

A quote should always state how long it is valid for, and the validity period should be realistic for the job: 14 or 30 days is common for freelance work, longer for a large project that needs approval, shorter where costs change quickly. After the validity period, the price no longer stands, and the client should ask for a fresh quote. State the validity on the face of the quote, not only in the covering email, so that the document itself carries the limit. If the client accepts after the validity period has passed, confirm the price in writing before you start the work, rather than assuming the old price still applies. If your costs have risen in the meantime, say so plainly, and offer a revised quote. A client who is told the price has changed before the work starts may be disappointed, but will not feel misled. A client who is told after the work is done will feel misled, and the relationship will suffer.

Changes after acceptance are the most common source of quote disputes, and the rule is simple: agree the change before you do the extra work. If the client asks for more — an extra deliverable, an extra revision round, a faster deadline, a change of scope — send a revised or supplementary quote that states what has changed, what the new price is, and that the original quote no longer applies to the changed part of the job, and wait for written acceptance. If the change is small, a short email exchange agreeing the change and the price is enough, but it must be in writing, and it must be before the work, not after it. If the change reduces the scope, say so too, and reduce the price or credit the difference on the invoice, so the client sees the reduction in plain figures. Never invoice for extra work the client never agreed to. It is the single most reliable way to lose a client and to delay payment, and no invoice, however well written, will make an unagreed charge acceptable.

Whether a quote is legally binding, and when it becomes binding, depends on the wording of the quote and on the law in your jurisdiction, and this guide cannot state the rule for every reader. In general, a quote that is accepted by the client can form, or become part of, a contract, and a quote that states a fixed price, a scope, and a validity period is more likely to be treated as an offer than a quote that is clearly labelled as an estimate, or that states that the price may change. For that reason, use the words carefully: if the price is fixed, say "fixed price". If it is an estimate, say "estimate", state the basis of the estimate, and state what could change it. Do not call a document an estimate and then treat it as a fixed price, or call it a fixed price and then charge more without agreement. If a quote is for a large job, or if you are unsure about the wording, ask a qualified adviser before you send it. The cost of advice before the job is small. The cost of a dispute after it is not. This section is general information, not legal advice, and it does not replace advice on your own circumstances.

Estimates, Fixed Prices, and Hourly Rates

Not every quote states a fixed price, and the form of the price should match the job. A fixed price suits a defined job: the deliverables are known, the scope is clear, and the price will not change unless the scope changes. Clients like fixed prices, because they can budget for them, and freelancers like them, because they reward efficiency. A fixed-price quote must be specific about what is included, because anything not listed will be argued about. An estimate suits a job whose scope cannot be fixed in advance: the work will take as long as it takes, or the final deliverable depends on findings along the way. An estimate must be labelled as an estimate, must state the basis — an hourly rate, a day rate, or a range — and must state a cap or a checkpoint if there is one, such as "estimated at 10 to 14 hours at the agreed rate, with a review at 10 hours". An estimate without a basis or a cap is not a quote. It is a guess, and the client will treat it as a promise. An hourly or day rate quote suits ongoing or open-ended work: state the rate, state what the rate covers, state how time will be recorded and reported, and state how often the client will be invoiced. Our guide to payment terms covers the invoicing rhythm for ongoing work, and a recurring invoice arrangement may suit a fixed monthly fee.

Whichever form you use, the invoice that follows must match it. A fixed-price quote produces a fixed-price invoice. An estimate produces an invoice for the time actually worked, with the time record attached or summarised, and the total within the estimated range or explained if it is not. An hourly-rate quote produces an invoice that shows the hours, the rate, and the dates, so the client can check it. A mismatch — an estimate followed by a fixed-price invoice, or a fixed-price quote followed by an invoice for extra hours the client never agreed to — is a query waiting to happen. State the pricing form on the quote, repeat it on the invoice, and keep the two documents consistent. That consistency is the whole job of a quote-to-invoice workflow, and it is what makes a client trust the next quote you send.

Common Mistakes With Quotes and Invoices

Titling a quote "Invoice". The client files it as a bill, the finance team queries it, and the job has not even started. Title every document correctly. Using an invoice number on a quote. Invoice numbers belong to invoices, in one sequence. A quote needs its own reference, in its own sequence. Quoting without a validity period. An open-ended quote can be accepted months later, at a price that no longer works. State the validity on the quote. Quoting a vague scope. "Design work, about £500" is not a quote. List the deliverables, the revisions, and the exclusions. Starting work without written acceptance. A verbal go-ahead is not a record. Wait for a written acceptance, however short. Invoicing before the work is done. An invoice that arrives before the work is a query, not a payment. Invoice on completion, or on the agreed date. Invoicing a different price from the quote. If the scope changed, agree the change and show it on the invoice. If it did not, the invoice must match the quote. Forgetting the deposit credit. A deposit already received must be credited on the final invoice, in plain figures. Charging the full amount again is an error the client will notice. Mixing up a quote, a proforma invoice, and a receipt. Each document has its own point in the job. Use the right one, and link to the proforma and receipt guides when you are unsure. Not keeping copies. A quote, an acceptance, and an invoice that are not filed together cannot be relied on. File the set, per client, per job, every time.

After Acceptance: Sending, Chasing, and Records

Once the quote is accepted and the work is done, the invoice process takes over, and the guides on this site cover each step: how to write the invoice, how to send it by email, how to chase an overdue invoice, and how to number it. The link between the quote and the invoice is the reference: put the quote reference on the invoice, and keep the quote, the acceptance, the invoice, and the payment record in one folder. If payment is late, the follow-up should refer to the invoice number, the amount, and the due date, and should attach the invoice again, so the client does not have to search for it. If the client queries the invoice, the quote is your answer: show the quote, the acceptance, and the agreed change, and the query will usually resolve itself. If the client is a repeat client, consider turning the accepted quote into a standing arrangement, with a recurring invoice for a fixed monthly fee, as explained in our recurring invoices guide, so that the quote-to-invoice cycle becomes a routine rather than a negotiation every month. And if the job was a one-off, keep the quote as a template for the next similar job: the wording, the exclusions, and the terms that worked can be reused, with the details changed, which makes the next quote faster and more consistent. A good quote is not just a document for one job. It is the start of a billing system that makes every later job easier to price, to agree, and to get paid for.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a quote and an invoice?

A quote is a price offer sent before the work starts: it tells the client what you will do and what it will cost, so the client can decide whether to accept. An invoice is a request for payment sent after the work has been done, or on an agreed date: it tells the client what was done, what is owed, and when and how to pay. A quote looks forward to work that may happen. An invoice looks back at work that has happened, and it is the document that starts the payment process.

Is a quote legally binding?

A quote can become binding once the client accepts it, depending on the wording of the quote and the law in your jurisdiction, so a quote should be written carefully and should state how long it is valid for, what it includes, and what would change the price. This guide is general information, not legal advice. If a quote is for a large job, or if you are unsure about the wording, ask a qualified adviser before you send it. Never promise in a quote what you cannot deliver at the stated price.

Can I invoice a client without sending a quote first?

Yes, in many cases you can. A quote is not required for every sale, and small, routine, or repeat jobs are often agreed by email or by a short message, with the invoice following the work. A written quote is most valuable when the price is significant, the scope is open to interpretation, the job is new, or the client needs approval from someone else before the work can start. Even when no formal quote is sent, confirm the price, the scope, and the payment terms in writing before the work starts, so the invoice never comes as a surprise.

What should I do if the work changes after I sent a quote?

Tell the client before you do the extra work, and agree the change in writing. Send a revised or supplementary quote that states what has changed, what the new price is, and that the original quote no longer applies to the changed part of the job, and wait for the client's acceptance before you proceed. Invoicing for extra work the client never agreed to is the most common cause of a payment dispute. A short written exchange agreeing the change protects both sides and keeps the relationship intact.
